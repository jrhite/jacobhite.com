ED 502 steps back from the classroom to look at the system around it: the laws,
policies and norms that decide what teaching is allowed to be. Four pieces of
that module are collected here — a research presentation on education law, the
disciplinary literacy activity, the cohort's IDEA resource, and the digital
citizenship activity.

### Education law and policy: Thai teacher licensing

The recording above is the research presentation. I live and expect to teach in
Thailand, so I researched the law that governs it there rather than one I would
only ever read about.

- **A law about Thai teachers, applied to foreign ones.** The 1999 National
  Education Act called for compulsory licensing; the 2003 Act created Khurusapha
  and made it mandatory. The statute never carved out an exemption for foreign
  teachers, but it was not written with them in mind either.
- **The waiver as the real route.** For two decades nearly every foreign teacher
  in a Thai government school worked on written permission to teach without a
  license — two years at a time, renewable up to three times. Six years, then the
  waiver is exhausted.
- **What the license pathway actually offers.** The 2023 regulation replaced the
  single five-year license with P, B and A tiers, and a foreigner pathway opened
  in 2024. Two cohorts of 500 have run against a reported waitlist near 10,000 —
  which makes the license a lottery rather than a pathway.
- **The position the law leaves a foreign teacher in.** A foreign national cannot
  hold a Thai teacher civil-service post, so the work is contract staff on
  fixed-term agreements tied to the school year. Even a full A-License does not
  open that door — no tenure, no pension track, and no seat where curriculum is
  decided.
- **The reflection.** The standard itself is right: teaching should be a licensed
  profession. What has not been built out is the capacity behind it, or a status
  the license can lead to.

Researching this changed what "the culture of schooling" means to me. The
staffing pattern in an international-track Thai classroom — who is permanent, who
is renewed annually, who sets the curriculum — is not an accident of any one
school. It is what this law produces.

### Activity 1: Disciplinary literacy in a computer science classroom

Literacy in a CS classroom works best when it is treated as *disciplinary*
literacy rather than generic reading strategies imported from English lessons.
Shanahan and Shanahan's (2008) core argument is that experts in different fields
read their texts in genuinely different ways, so the strategies have to be
specific to how computer scientists read and write — not a one-size-fits-all
"every teacher a teacher of reading."

For computer science the texts are code, error messages, documentation,
specifications and diagrams, and the reading practices are tracing, decomposition
and abstraction. Concretely:

- **A "predict" starter.** Open the lesson with a five- to eight-line program on
  the board. Students write down what they think the output is before anyone runs
  it.
- **Annotated code reading.** Students mark up a printout, highlighting variables,
  control flow, and the points where state changes.
- **Parsons problems.** Scrambled lines of correct code to be reassembled
  (Parsons & Haden, 2006). They strip out the syntax burden and force attention
  onto structure and sequence, while exposing students only to well-written code.
- **Line-by-line tracing tables.** A column per variable, a row per iteration —
  run as a routine, not a one-off.

### Activity 3: Teaching digital citizenship

#### Why teach digital citizenship?

There are a number of reasons to teach digital citizenship: online safety, being
respectful of people with different viewpoints, telling fact from fiction online,
using technology to engage in civil action, balancing time online and off, and
knowing how to stay safe and how to make a space safe for others (ISTE, 2019).

AI belongs in that list now. It is advancing faster every day, and the ways the
general public, students and professionals apply it are still expanding. Because
the technology is moving so fast, our use of it in learning — from both the
student's side and the teacher's — is still nascent. Teachers need to stay on top
of it and start covering it more comprehensively as part of what digital
citizenship means, and as part of what healthy, meaningful learning looks like.

#### Teaching what plagiarism is, and how to avoid it

I would start by defining it and giving concrete examples of what it looks like:

- copying and pasting from a source, online or off, without citing it
- paying someone to do your homework
- using AI to do the work outright, rather than to help you understand it

Then I would teach students how to properly cite, quote and use sources in their
classroom work (MORKSENSEI, 2020). Then — and this is the part that actually
changes behavior — why we would want to avoid it in the first place.

- **Plagiarism hurts the student most of all.** Copying skips the learning: the
  problem solving, the creativity of arriving at your own answer, and the chance
  to make mistakes and learn what mistakes are worth. Mistakes are not a bad
  thing. They are the growth opportunity.
- **It hurts the teacher's read on their own teaching.** Plagiarized work makes it
  impossible to judge whether a concept landed, for that student or for the class.
- **It distorts grading for everyone else.** Plagiarized work is often taken from
  sources well beyond the level of the class, which moves the scale for students
  who did their own.

#### Secondary computer science

**Socially responsible online behavior, and why students need practice.** It means
interacting in digital spaces in ways that respect the dignity, privacy and
wellbeing of others, so that students feel safe voicing their ideas openly. It
includes communicating respectfully in disagreement, protecting personal
information, crediting other people's work, thinking before sharing, and speaking
up — or getting an adult — when witnessing cyberbullying. It is the digital
extension of citizenship: behind every username is a real person, and online
actions have real consequences.

Students need structured opportunities to practice these behaviors, not just hear
about them, because digital citizenship is a skill, not a fact. Practicing in a
supervised space builds habits that transfer to the rest of their digital lives.

**Discord as the safe community platform.** A teacher-administered private server
can be configured for safety: invite-only access, teacher-held moderator
permissions, member-to-member direct messages disabled, and separate channels for
coursework, collaboration and casual community-building. Quieter students can
participate in writing rather than by speaking up in class, and the persistent
chat history lets the teacher monitor interactions and address problems early.

- **Example activity 1 — co-authoring the community guidelines.** Before the server goes
  live, students draft its rules in small groups: what respectful disagreement
  looks like, how to critique a classmate's code without belittling them, what to
  do when someone posts something hurtful. The class negotiates a final version
  and pins it as a living document they agreed to, rather than rules imposed on
  them, which makes the norms a shared responsibility.
- **Example activity 2 — an introductions channel with structured responses.** Once the
  guidelines are pinned, students put them into practice immediately: everyone
  posts a short introduction in `#introductions` — name, an outside interest, and
  something they hope to build this year — following the norms the class just
  agreed on for what is appropriate to share in a semi-public space. Each student
  then replies warmly to at least two classmates they do not know well, with the
  guidelines as the standard for a respectful, welcoming response. Sequencing the
  two this way means students are not writing rules in the abstract; their first
  real interactions on the server are a live application of the community they
  defined, with the teacher modeling it by replying to every post.

**Guiding safe, legal and ethical use of online resources.** I would build three
habits.

- **Attribution.** Code is intellectual property, so copying from Stack Overflow
  without citation is plagiarism. Students cite sources in code comments and use
  properly licensed media.
- **Verification.** Wikipedia is a starting point, not an endpoint. Claims get
  traced to a source and cross-checked.
- **Disclosure.** AI may be used to explain a concept or an error, but all
  assistance must be disclosed, and submitting AI-generated code as your own is
  academic dishonesty. AI can be confidently wrong, so students must be able to
  explain every line they submit — if you cannot explain it, you do not get credit
  for it. And no personal information goes into an AI tool or a public forum.
