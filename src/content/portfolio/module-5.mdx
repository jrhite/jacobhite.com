### Unit 1, Activity 1: Understanding formative assessments

A reflection on how a recorded secondary lesson checks for understanding, and
how I would make those checks accessible, record them, and use the data.

I watched the Secondary (Online) lesson video, an asynchronous high school lesson in which Mr. Stewart teaches how laws are made in the United States (Moreland University, 2021). I chose it because it is the closest match to my grade band and subject, Computer Science 7–12. A great deal of computer science is learned online and at the student's own pace, so the assessment problems of an asynchronous lesson are the ones I expect to face.

#### What information or ideas were evaluated during the assessment?

The lesson checks four kinds of understanding:

- **Prior knowledge.** It opens with a pre-assessment: which branch of government has the power to make laws?
- **Key vocabulary.** Students define the lesson's terms (such as *bill* and *public policy*) with a dictionary before the lecture starts.
- **The process of how a bill becomes a law.** Mid-lesson, students recall the sequence from memory: an idea, a bill introduced in either the House or the Senate, committee work, a floor vote, the other chamber, then the president's signature or veto. They also check who can repeal a law (only Congress; the Supreme Court can rule a law unconstitutional, but that is not a repeal).
- **Application.** In an online simulation, students apply what they learned about districts, constituents and demographics to steer a bill through to a vote.

#### What activity or technology did students use to demonstrate their knowledge or skills?

The whole lesson is a recorded screen-share with pause points, plus readings and a primer video on the class learning platform. Inside that, students show what they know in five ways:

1. **Pause-and-answer questions** for the pre-assessment and vocabulary.
2. **An oral retrieval check.** Mr. Stewart asks students to look away from the screen and recall each step of the process, then pauses before confirming the answer.
3. **A teach-back.** Students explain the process to a family member, or aloud to themselves, using sentence frames on the slide.
4. **A bill-to-law simulation game.** Students pick a district, study its demographics, survey constituents and introduce a bill. They submit two screenshots: the final results (whether the bill passed, constituent support, re-election chances) and a certificate showing the date, district, bill, correct survey predictions and time spent.
5. **Reflection questions and a two-question exit ticket**, one of which asks what the lesson left out.

One thing stood out: most of these checks are self-checks. The only evidence that actually reaches the teacher is the two game screenshots, the reflection and the exit ticket. The pre-assessment and the oral recall check give students feedback, but they give the teacher no data.

#### How might you make this accessible to students with language barriers and those with special needs?

I would assume at least one English language learner (ELL) and one student with an IEP in the class. Most of the supports below are **accommodations**: they change *how* a student learns and shows learning, not *what* they are expected to learn. A **modification** changes the expectation itself, so I would use one only when a student's IEP calls for it (Belsky, 2025).

**For an ELL student** (for example, a grade 9 student at an intermediate English level):

- Edit the video's auto-generated captions and post a clean transcript. The automatic captions contain errors that would confuse a language learner.
- Give a short bilingual glossary of the key terms, with an icon or diagram for each, and allow a home-language dictionary during the vocabulary step.
- Add a word bank to the sentence frames that are already in the lesson.
- Provide the bill-to-law process as a flowchart. In my computer science classes, flowcharts are already a familiar way to show a process.
- Let the student pair with a peer for the game, and turn on the browser's text-to-speech for game text.
- Accept the exit ticket as a short audio recording, or written first in the home language and then in English.

**For a student with special needs** (for example, a student with ADHD and a reading disability):

- Split the 16-minute video into short segments, each ending with one quick check, so attention is reset often.
- Offer text-to-speech for the readings and the game, and extended time for the game.
- Give a checklist of exactly what to submit (two screenshots), since missing one means replaying the game.
- Let the student answer the reflection questions orally.
- Only if the IEP requires a modification: a shorter process to sequence (for example, four steps instead of seven) or a reduced vocabulary list (Belsky, 2025).

#### How might you gather and record data from this assessment?

I would turn the self-checks into data I can see:

- **Pre-assessment and exit ticket** as a short, auto-graded Google Forms quiz, so each answer lands in a spreadsheet by student and by question.
- **Game results** copied from each certificate into the same sheet: bill passed (yes or no), constituent support (%), survey predictions correct, and time spent.
- **Teach-back and reflection** scored with a simple three-point rubric on the accuracy and order of the bill-to-law steps. Students could record the teach-back as a short audio clip.
- Each question **tagged to a learning objective**, so the sheet shows mastery per objective, not only a total score.
- Columns noting **which accommodations** each student used, so I can see whether the supports are working.

Using several sources (quiz answers, game results, rubric scores and my own notes) gives a fuller picture of each student than any one score (Holloman, 2022).

#### How might you use the data to inform instruction?

- **Compare the baseline with the exit ticket.** The pre-assessment is my baseline; the exit ticket shows growth against it (Holloman, 2022).
- **Reteach by item.** If many students miss the question on who can repeal a law, the next lesson opens with a five-minute contrast of "repealed by Congress" versus "ruled unconstitutional by the Supreme Court."
- **Group by need.** Students who missed the sequence get a card-sort of the steps in a small group; students who mastered it trace a real bill's path through Congress as an extension.
- **Read the game data closely.** A student with very little time spent and few correct predictions probably clicked through the game. That calls for a quick conference, not a grade.
- **Use students' feedback.** The exit ticket question about what the lesson left out tells me what to add next time.
- **Monitor progress over time for students who need it.** For a student receiving extra support, I would graph at least six to eight data points before deciding whether the support is working. If the four most recent points all fall below the goal line, I change the instruction (The IRIS Center, n.d.).

In my own computer science classes, the same pattern works for a lesson on algorithms: a one-question pre-assessment, a flowchart of the process, an auto-graded exit ticket, and a coding simulation whose results students submit as evidence.

#### The assessments I chose

**Formal:** easyCBM Proficient Reading (grade 8 form, Lite edition), taken
online, for a comprehension score and an item-level report (easyCBM, n.d.).

**Informal:** a one-minute oral reading fluency probe on an original computer
science passage about loops, scored for words correct per minute and accuracy,
rated for prosody with the Multidimensional Fluency Scale, and followed by a
one-minute retell (Zutell & Rasinski, 1991; Hasbrouck & Tindal, 2017).

I paired them on purpose. The formal measure tells me how well each student
comprehends; the informal probe on a domain-specific text helps explain why, by
showing whether choppy or slow reading of technical text is getting in the way.
A single probe is one data point, so I treat it as a starting point and gather
six to eight before deciding anything about one student (The IRIS Center,
n.d.). Oral reading probes are most useful alongside comprehension questions,
which is why the two go together here (UNESCO Institute for Statistics, 2016).

#### Plan: fluency for comprehension in computer science texts

**Practice:** repeated reading of a short computer science text, followed by summarizing. Repeated reading builds automaticity and phrasing, which frees attention for meaning (Rasinski, 2012); summarizing is a comprehension strategy recommended for adolescent readers (Kamil et al., 2008).

**Text:** a 150-word paragraph from the documentation for a Python feature we are about to use (e.g., the for statement), the kind of domain-specific text students must read on their own as programmers (Shanahan & Shanahan, 2008).

**Steps (three 15-minute small-group sessions in one week):**

1. Pre-teach five technical terms (e.g., iterate, sequence, element, range, indentation). Students say each term aloud, clap its syllables and add it to a Frayer model.

2. I model a fluent reading of the paragraph, showing how to phrase code inside a sentence (for example, reading `for i in range(5)` as one unit).

3. Choral reading, then partner repeated reading, three times each session. Partners mark phrase breaks with slashes and give one piece of feedback with the Multidimensional Fluency Scale.

4. Each student writes a one-sentence summary (“This paragraph explains… so that…”) and then runs the code example to check that the summary matches what the code actually does.

5. Extended discussion: students compare summaries and settle on the most accurate one, justifying it with words from the text and the program's output.

**Measuring progress:** a one-minute WCPM probe and a retell on a new, similar passage at the end of each week, graphed against a goal line. After six to eight data points I will raise the goal, keep going, or change the approach, using the four-point method (The IRIS Center, n.d.). Comprehension is judged by the accuracy of each student's summary.

#### Technology in the assessments (Secondary Computer Science methods)

- **Informal assessments that technology accomplishes or enriches:** retells
  and summaries recorded or typed into a Google Form; auto-scored question sets
  in Google Forms or Kahoot!; fluency probes recorded as audio so they can be
  re-scored and tracked over time; "explain your code" walkthroughs recorded
  with Loom or Screencastify; vocabulary rating scales as a Google Forms grid;
  Frayer models, concept maps and card sorts in Google Slides, Miro or FigJam.
  Phonemic awareness and phonics screeners stay face to face.
- **Collecting data:** audio recordings of each probe (a phone voice memo or
  Vocaroo), and speech-to-text for a rough transcript.
- **Analyzing data:** easyCBM's item reports and progress graphs, plus a Google
  Sheet with one row per student, one column per probe date and a goal line.
  Students can analyze their own reading data with a short Python script.
- **Timely feedback:** instant Google Forms scores, same-day comments in Docs
  or Classroom, short audio comments that English learners can replay, and code
  review in GitHub Classroom or Replit that checks a summary against what the
  code actually does.
