### Unit 2, Activity 1: Language acquisition, cognitive development, and literacy in the digital age

One influential theory of second language acquisition is Stephen Krashen's Input
Hypothesis, part of his Monitor Model. Krashen is often cited alongside researchers
like Jim Cummins and Catherine Snow as foundational to how educators understand
second-language development (Robertson & Ford, n.d.). D'Souza and Padmanabha (2024)
note that scholars still debate whether Krashen's framework is a full theory or a
set of hypotheses, since it rests heavily on the claim that comprehensible input
alone drives acquisition. The core idea is that learners progress most when input is
pitched just beyond their current level but still understandable through context or
simplified speech. Krashen also proposed an "affective filter," where anxiety or low
motivation can block acquisition even with strong input, making a low-stress
environment a precondition for learning.

Krashen's ideas align with the stages of second language acquisition, moving from
Preproduction (the "silent period") through Early Production, Speech Emergence,
Intermediate Fluency and Advanced Fluency (Robertson & Ford, n.d.). During the
silent period, students absorb input before producing language, matching Krashen's
claim that comprehension precedes production. This also fits the definition of
sequential language acquisition, which assumes a first language is already
established and builds new vocabulary on top of it (American Speech-Language-Hearing
Association, as cited in Reading Rockets, n.d.-a). A newcomer's silence, then, is
expected processing, not disengagement.

#### What this looks like in a computer science classroom

The theory says pitch the input just past the learner and keep the anxiety down.
In a grades 9-12 programming room, that turns into five concrete moves.

- **Make the input recoverable from something other than the prose.** Documentation
  and error messages are the texts my students read most, and both are dense. Every
  doc paragraph I assign is paired with a runnable snippet, so meaning can be
  recovered by running the code and changing a value when the sentence alone does
  not land. The example carries the comprehensible part.
- **Let a silent student show understanding without speaking.** During the silent
  period a newcomer can trace a loop on paper, predict a program's output before we
  run it, or reassemble a Parsons problem from scrambled lines. All three make the
  thinking visible and gradeable while production is still emerging, which means I
  am not mistaking silence for not knowing.
- **Give the language, not just the task.** Sentence frames for code review carry
  the register: "I expected ___ but I saw ___", "this function returns ___
  because ___". The student contributes the reasoning; the frame supplies the
  syntax, which is the affective filter in action rather than in theory.
- **Pre-teach the words that block comprehension.** Direction verbs in every
  prompt (create, return, iterate, review) and the pairs students confuse
  (variable and value, argument and parameter) go up before the task, and cognates
  get pointed at directly, since *function* and *función* or *variable* and
  *variable* are free vocabulary for a Spanish-speaking student.
- **Let the home language do the planning.** Pairs may design and argue in whichever
  language they think fastest in; the artifact that ships, the README and the commit
  message, is in English. The cognitive work is not gated on the second language,
  but the written product still builds it.

#### Where the theory runs out

Comprehensible input is a good account of why a scaffolded classroom works, and a
poor guide to what to do on Tuesday.

The central objection is that *i+1* cannot be operationally defined. There is no
way to measure a student's current level precisely enough to pitch input one step
beyond it, which also makes the claim difficult to falsify (Gregg, 1984). As a
teacher I get no decision procedure from it: it tells me to scaffold, not which
scaffold.

It also underweights output. Swain (1985) argued that producing language forces
syntactic processing that comprehension alone never demands, and computer science
makes that gap unusually visible. A student can trace code they cannot yet write,
and follow a design discussion they cannot yet join. Waiting for production to
emerge on its own leaves that unaddressed, which is why the moves above ask for
output early and support it, rather than deferring it.

The affective-filter argument can also be pushed too far. Read strictly, it
discourages correction, but the meta-analytic evidence favours explicit,
form-focused instruction over purely implicit exposure (Norris & Ortega, 2000). Low
anxiety is a condition for learning, not a substitute for teaching. And
conversational fluency arrives years ahead of academic language (Cummins, 2008): a
student who chats easily will still not absorb *parameter*, *idempotent* or *race
condition* from context, because a CS classroom's register is technical and rare.
Those have to be taught outright.

The risk that matters most is clinical. Treating a long silence as expected
processing is right most of the time, and wrong in the case where it is masking a
language disorder or a learning difference. That is the judgement call the
[referral flowchart in Module 2](/portfolio/module-2#u3a1) exists to make, and
Krashen's framework gives no way to tell the two apart.
