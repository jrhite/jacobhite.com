### Activity 1: Supporting ELL and MLL students
<VideoEmbed
  src="/media/supporting-ell-mll-students.mp4"
  poster="/media/supporting-ell-mll-students-poster.jpg"
  title="Supporting ELL & MLL Students"
  duration="4:19"
  aspect="1792 / 992"
  caption="A narrated walkthrough of three practices for English language learners and multilingual learners: scaffolding discussion, treating the home language as a resource, and teaching academic vocabulary explicitly."
/>

- **Why scaffold classroom discussion.** Scaffolding is temporary support matched
  to a student's stage — sentence frames, visuals, modeled examples, vocabulary
  previews, graphic organizers — not a permanently easier curriculum. Grade-level
  expectations stay where they are; what changes is how reachable they are.
- **The home language as a classroom resource.** Bilingual proficiency tracks with
  stronger achievement in English reading and math, and reading skills transfer
  from a first language to a second. Even a monolingual classroom can use this:
  ask how the home language compares to English, let newcomers read aloud in it,
  treat it as a communication tool rather than an obstacle.
- **Academic vocabulary, taught explicitly.** Pre-teach the words most likely to
  block comprehension, match the support to the student's stage, connect new terms
  to cognates and background knowledge, and give low-stakes practice before
  anything is graded.

### Activity 2: Evidence-based instructional practices
<VideoEmbed
  src="/media/evidence-based-instruction.mp4"
  poster="/media/evidence-based-instruction-poster.jpg"
  title="Evidence-Based Instruction Practices"
  duration="5:40"
  aspect="16 / 9"
  caption="What makes a practice evidence-based, and three that clear the bar — explicit instruction, feedback and checking for understanding, and cooperative learning — each with what it looks like in my own 9–12 classroom."
/>

A practice earns the label when the evidence behind it is objective, reliable,
valid, systematic and refereed — not when it is merely popular. Three that clear
that bar, and what each looks like in a 9–12 room:

- **Explicit instruction.** Open with a short review of prior learning, present
  new material in small steps with guided practice after each one, model with
  think-alouds, and aim for roughly an 80% success rate during guided practice
  before fading the scaffolds. I model the lab write-up on the board, thinking out
  loud, before students draft their own.
- **Feedback and checking for understanding.** Feedback has to arrive early enough
  that students can still act on it. A three-question entry slip opens class and I
  re-ask the missed question the next day, so the gap surfaces on Tuesday instead
  of on the unit test. Low-stakes retrieval finds what re-reading hides.
- **Cooperative learning.** Structured collaboration, not "get into groups." Every
  member has a defined job — recorder, reporter, skeptic — and Think-Pair-Share
  runs before whole-class discussion so no one coasts. Peer-based instruction is
  among the 28 evidence-based practices identified by the NCAEP, so it holds up in
  inclusive settings too.

The through-line is one assumption: all students can succeed when instruction is
built on what the research actually shows.

### Activity 3: Literacy tools hiding in the CS toolchain
<VideoEmbed
  src="/media/cs-literacy-tools.mp4"
  poster="/media/cs-literacy-tools-poster.jpg"
  title="Literacy Tools Hiding in the CS Toolchain"
  duration="4:34"
  aspect="16 / 9"
  caption="A tour of the TypeScript compiler, ESLint, Prettier and Replit as literacy tools — what each one does for orthographic awareness and writing in grades 9–12."
/>

Secondary computer science does not need literacy software bolted on. The tools
students already run are literacy tools if you point them at literacy:

- **The TypeScript compiler and ESLint** work as a spell checker for identifiers.
  `no-undef` flags `totalScroe` as never declared, which nine times out of ten is
  a misspelling — orthographic awareness with an instant, impersonal reader.
- **Prettier** settles formatting so critique can be about meaning rather than
  whitespace, the same way a style sheet frees an essay conference to be about
  the argument.
- **Replit** gives everyone the same environment, so a student's first experience
  of reading and writing code is not a setup problem.

This is the same argument as the disciplinary literacy work on
[Module 2](/portfolio/module-2): the reading and writing practices belong to the
discipline, and so do the tools.
