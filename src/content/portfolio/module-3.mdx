### Unit 2, Activity 1: Language acquisition, cognitive development, and literacy in the digital age

One influential theory of second language acquisition is Stephen Krashen's Input
Hypothesis, part of his Monitor Model. Krashen is often cited alongside researchers
like Jim Cummins and Catherine Snow as foundational to how educators understand
second-language development (Robertson & Ford, n.d.). D'Souza and Padmanabha (2024)
note that scholars still debate whether Krashen's framework is a full theory or a
set of hypotheses, since it rests heavily on the claim that comprehensible input
alone drives acquisition. The core idea is that learners progress most when input is
pitched just beyond their current level but still understandable through context or
simplified speech. Krashen also proposed an "affective filter," where anxiety or low
motivation can block acquisition even with strong input, making a low-stress
environment a precondition for learning.

Krashen's ideas align with the stages of second language acquisition, moving from
Preproduction (the "silent period") through Early Production, Speech Emergence,
Intermediate Fluency and Advanced Fluency (Robertson & Ford, n.d.). During the
silent period, students absorb input before producing language, matching Krashen's
claim that comprehension precedes production. This also fits the definition of
sequential language acquisition, which assumes a first language is already
established and builds new vocabulary on top of it (American Speech-Language-Hearing
Association, as cited in Reading Rockets, n.d.-a). A newcomer's silence, then, is
expected processing, not disengagement.

#### What this looks like in a computer science classroom

The theory says pitch the input just past the learner and keep the anxiety down.
In a grades 9-12 programming room, that turns into three concrete moves.

- **Make the input recoverable from something other than the prose.**
  Documentation and error messages will be the texts my students read most, and
  both are dense. Every doc paragraph I assign will be paired with a runnable
  snippet, so meaning can be recovered by running the code and changing a value
  when the sentence alone does not land. The example carries the comprehensible
  part.
- **Let a silent student show understanding without speaking.** During the silent
  period a newcomer can trace a loop on paper, predict a program's output before
  we run it, or reassemble a Parsons problem from scrambled lines. All three make
  the thinking visible and gradeable while production is still emerging, which
  means I will not be mistaking silence for not knowing.
- **Give the language, not just the task.** Sentence frames for code review carry
  the register: "I expected ___ but I saw ___", "this function returns ___ because
  ___". The student contributes the semantics and reasoning; the frame supplies
  the syntax, which is the affective filter in action rather than in theory.

#### Where the theory runs out

Comprehensible input explains why a scaffolded classroom works, but not what to do
on Tuesday.

*i+1* cannot be operationally defined: no one can measure a student's level
precisely enough to pitch input one step beyond it, which also makes the claim hard
to falsify (Gregg, 1984). It says scaffold, not which scaffold.

It underweights output. Producing language forces syntactic processing that
comprehension never demands (Swain, 1985), and a student can trace code they cannot
write. Waiting for production to emerge leaves that gap unaddressed.

Read strictly, the affective filter discourages correction, but the evidence favors
explicit, form-focused instruction (Norris & Ortega, 2000). And conversational
fluency arrives years ahead of academic language (Cummins, 2008), so *parameter* and
*race condition* will not be absorbed from context.

The sharpest limit is clinical. A long silence is expected processing most of the
time, and sometimes a language disorder. Krashen cannot tell them apart, which is
what the [referral flowchart in Module 2](/portfolio/module-2#u3a1) is for.

### Unit 3, Activity 1: Technology use in education

The tool I want to take further is generative AI, and specifically the coding
assistants students already have open in another tab. I will be teaching
secondary computer science, grades 9 through 12, so roughly ages fourteen to
eighteen, and this will not be a hypothetical technology by the time I get
there. It is already in the room, ahead of me.

Most classroom technology helps you learn to do something. A simulation, a video,
an IDE: each of them still leaves the doing to you. Generative AI is the first
tool most students have met that will also just do the thing, and often do it
better than they can. That is what makes its benefits and its challenges so
hard to pull apart, because they are the same property seen from two sides.

#### The question underneath: where does the line go?

I do not know how an internal combustion engine works. I drive perfectly well
anyway. Learning the thermodynamics would be a hobby, not a competence I need,
and nobody thinks less of me for that. Abstraction is not a failure of education,
it is what makes any of this possible, and my own field is built on the same
move: I write TypeScript without reading the compiler, open a socket without
implementing TCP, and allocate memory without writing the collector. Every one of
those is an engine I never open.

So when a student asks why they should learn to write the loop when the model
writes it faster and cleaner, they are not being lazy. They are making the
abstraction argument, and it is a good argument. The question is not whether to
draw a line, because everyone draws one. The question is where, and on what
basis.

Two things settle it for me. The first is that the abstraction leaks. Spolsky's
(2002) law of leaky abstractions holds that every non-trivial abstraction leaks:
it works until it does not, and when it fails you need to know what was
underneath. Driving is an unusually clean case, because the engine's interface is
stable and someone else, a mechanic, is accountable when it is not. Generated
code leaks constantly and confidently. It compiles, it reads well, and it has an
off-by-one in it.

The second is Bainbridge's (1983) ironies of automation, written about aircraft
cockpits forty years ago and describing the classroom I am walking into
exactly. Automating the
routine parts of a job leaves the human only the hard parts, the ones that need
the most skill, while removing the everyday practice that built that skill. The
operator ends up responsible for catching failures in a system they are no longer
fluent enough to monitor.

Put those together and the line stops being a matter of taste. **You may abstract
away what you are not accountable for verifying.** I am not accountable for my
engine, so I am allowed to drive in ignorance. My students will be accountable
for the code they ship, so they may use the model, and they will have to be able
to read what it hands back, say what it does, and say what happens if it is
wrong. That
is a far lower bar than writing it unaided. It is nowhere near zero.

#### Benefits

A model is a tutor with infinite patience and no schedule, which is the closest
anyone has come to offering Bloom's (1984) two sigma problem a practical answer:
the gap between a student in a class of thirty and the same student with a
one-to-one tutor. It will explain the same pointer diagram nine times at eleven
at night without sighing. For a multilingual learner it sits on top of an error
message, which is the most hostile text in my subject, and makes that input
comprehensible on demand, which is the Krashen argument from Unit 2 of this
module arriving as a product feature. It lowers the floor for a first working
program, and a first working program is where motivation comes from. On my side
of the desk it will draft three differentiated versions of a problem set in the
time any one of them would otherwise take.

#### Challenges

The evidence is not that AI is bad for learners. It is that AI is best for the
learners who need it least. Prather et al. (2024) watched novices work with
generative AI and found the gap between strong and struggling students widening
rather than closing: students who could already evaluate the output got faster,
and students who could not were led further astray by fluent code they had no way
to judge. Fan et al. (2025) name the mechanism metacognitive laziness, the
offloading of the monitoring and regulating work to the model, which feels like
learning and is not. Lee et al. (2025) found the same shape in adults at work,
where higher confidence in the tool predicted less critical thinking and higher
confidence in one's own expertise predicted more.

Three further costs I take seriously. **Assessment:** the take-home programming
assignment is finished as a measure of individual capability, and pretending
otherwise will be my failure, not the students'. **Equity:** the paid tiers are
meaningfully better than the free ones, so a classroom that quietly assumes AI
imports an income gradient. **Privacy:** this is the one with law attached to it,
and it gets its own section below.

#### Co-learning: how my class will find and vet a new tool

The International Society for Technology in Education (ISTE, n.d.-a) puts this in
the educator standards as 2.4.b, collaborating and co-learning with students to
discover and use new digital resources and to troubleshoot technology issues,
which is the standard I am planning against here rather than a habit I invented.
Four methods:

- **A student-led tool review, on rotation.** Every two weeks a pair will take
  one candidate tool and give the class a five-minute verdict against a rubric
  the class wrote itself: what does it do that we cannot already do, what data
  does it want, who owns what we put into it, what does it cost once the trial
  ends, and what breaks if it disappears next term. The resulting list belongs to
  the class, not to me.
- **I will evaluate live, and badly.** A tool I have never used goes on the
  projector and I fail at it in front of the class, narrating as I go. The point
  is to model not-knowing as a normal professional state rather than a thing
  teachers hide.
- **A standing invitation.** If a student has found something, they bring it to
  the review and defend it. I expect that to be where most of what we adopt comes
  from, because they are downstream of far more software than I am.
- **Whoever reviews it, writes it up.** Each adopted tool will get a paragraph in
  the class handbook under the reviewer's name, which makes the list an artifact
  the class owns rather than a handout from me.

#### Investigating safely: student safety and privacy

Investigation is the part that needs guardrails, because a new tool is an unknown
counterparty. What I will do, in order:

- **Sandbox first.** A new tool meets a throwaway project in a class account
  before it touches graded work or anything personal.
- **The public-only rule.** Nothing goes into an unvetted tool that is not
  already public. No names, no student IDs, no photographs, no addresses, no
  credentials, and nobody else's work. We will practice on a fake dataset I
  maintain for the purpose, so the habit is built before the stakes are real.
- **Three questions of the terms of service,** read together on the projector the
  first few times: what do they collect, do they train on it, and can I get it
  deleted. The Privacy Technical Assistance Center (2014) guidance is written for
  exactly this reading, and reading a privacy policy is a disciplinary literacy
  skill, which is the [Module 2](/portfolio/module-2#u1a1) argument again.
- **Account hygiene.** School account only, never a personal email, never signing
  in through a personal Google account, and I will never ask a student to create
  an account somewhere I would not want their name printed.
- **The legal floor, taught rather than assumed.** FERPA governs their education
  records and the protection follows the data to the vendor (Family Educational
  Rights and Privacy Act, 1974). COPPA governs users under 13, which will not be
  my roster but is very much the audience for the apps my students will build
  (Children's Online Privacy Protection Act, 1998). The tools have age gates of
  their own too: several assistants require 18, or 13 with parental consent, and
  a class where I quietly overlooked that would be teaching something I did not
  intend.
- **Five minutes as the malicious user.** On every tool we adopt: where does this
  leak, who else can see this link, what happens if I put someone else's name in.

#### When it breaks: engaging students in problem solving

New tools break, and the moment they break is the lesson. My students will get
one protocol, and it is deliberately the same one whether the thing that broke
is a compiler, an API, or a tool the class adopted last week. It is Pólya's (1945)
four stages fitted to a toolchain, and ISTE (n.d.-b) makes troubleshooting a
student standard rather than the teacher's job: standard 1.1.d asks students to
choose, use and troubleshoot current technologies and to transfer that knowledge
to technologies nobody has taught them.

![A six-step infographic titled "Stuck? Six Moves." for secondary computer science, grades 9 to 12. Three phases: Understand it, covering step 1, say what broke, and step 2, read all of it; Narrow it down, covering step 3, change one thing, and step 4, search the error; Ask and record, covering step 5, ask a real question, which forbids pasting passwords, keys, or personal information into an unvetted tool, and step 6, log the fix in the class troubleshooting log. A footer reads: still stuck after 20 minutes, go back to step 3 with a smaller case, then bring your step 1 sentence to a person.](/media/problem-solving-process.png)

[Download the original file (.svg, 10 KB) →](/media/problem-solving-process.svg)

Around the poster will sit four classroom routines.

- **The stuck note is the price of asking.** Before a student asks anyone,
  including me, they write down what they expected, what happened, and what they
  have already tried. Writing that note solves the problem outright often enough
  to be worth the friction, which is rubber duck debugging under a different name
  (Hunt & Thomas, 1999).
- **Ask a partner, then the class, then me,** and no more than twenty minutes
  alone before the first of those. Being stuck is normal. Being stuck in silence
  for a period is a routine failure, not a character one.
- **A class troubleshooting log,** symptom, cause and fix, that everyone can
  write to. It turns a solved problem into a shared artifact and gives step 6 a
  reason to exist.
- **I will grade the process, not just the fix.** The stuck note and the log
  entry get assessed. A student who documented a failure they never solved has
  done the work I actually care about.

The one thing I will not do is take the keyboard and fix it at the board. That
resolves the problem and teaches that the teacher is the debugger.

#### Reflection: would I use this in my classroom?

Yes, under four conditions, and the conditions carry more of the answer than the
yes does.

1. **AI is allowed and disclosed, never hidden.** Disclosure will be a line in
   the commit message: what I asked for, and what I changed afterward. Hidden use
   is the thing I am against, not use.
2. **Assessment moves to what the model cannot do for you.** Explaining generated
   code line by line out loud, writing tests that challenge the requirement
   rather than confirm the implementation, and defending the design in
   conversation.
3. **There will be deliberately unassisted stretches.** Not because assistance is
   cheating, but because the fluency you need to supervise the model is built by
   working without it, which is the Bainbridge point turned into a schedule.
4. **The privacy floor is not negotiable,** and neither is the guidance around
   all of this. Kirschner et al. (2006) is my reason for not simply turning
   students loose with a powerful tool and trusting discovery to do the rest.

That is where I draw my own line, and I will say so to the class rather than
pretend the rule came down from somewhere. I do not read the compiler. I do read
every line the model writes for me, because when it is wrong at two in the
morning, it is my name on it. The U.S. Department of Education's (2023) framing is
the one I keep returning to: keep humans in the loop, and design the classroom so
that staying in the loop is the easy path.

And the honest answer to my own question, where each person draws the line, is
that it moves. It moves with what you are responsible for. A student writing a
toy program can afford an ignorance I cannot afford shipping a login form.
Teaching that the line moves, and teaching students to notice when it has moved
under them, is worth more than any fixed policy I could hand out.
