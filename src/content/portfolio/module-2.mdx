ED 502 steps back from the classroom to look at the system around it: the laws,
policies and norms that decide what teaching is allowed to be. Six pieces of
that module are collected here, in unit and activity order — disciplinary
literacy, the laws and policies research presentation, digital citizenship,
language development, promoting literacy, and the cohort's IDEA resource.

### Unit 1, Activity 1: Disciplinary literacy in a computer science classroom

Literacy in a CS classroom works best when it is treated as *disciplinary*
literacy rather than generic reading strategies imported from English lessons.
Shanahan and Shanahan's (2008) core argument is that experts in different fields
read their texts in genuinely different ways, so the strategies have to be
specific to how computer scientists read and write — not a one-size-fits-all
"every teacher a teacher of reading."

For computer science the texts are code, error messages, documentation,
specifications and diagrams, and the reading practices are tracing, decomposition
and abstraction. Concretely:

- **A "predict" starter.** Open the lesson with a five- to eight-line program on
  the board. Students write down what they think the output is before anyone runs
  it.
- **Annotated code reading.** Students mark up a printout, highlighting variables,
  control flow, and the points where state changes.
- **Parsons problems.** Scrambled lines of correct code to be reassembled
  (Parsons & Haden, 2006). They strip out the syntax burden and force attention
  onto structure and sequence, while exposing students only to well-written code.
- **Line-by-line tracing tables.** A column per variable, a row per iteration —
  run as a routine, not a one-off.

The vocabulary side of this lesson goes into the cohort's shared Literacy Guide.
The guide has one home on this site rather than a copy of the link on every module
it touches — it is on the [Literacy](/portfolio/literacy) page.

I live and expect to teach in Thailand, so I researched the law that governs it
there rather than one I would only ever read about.

- **A law about Thai teachers, applied to foreign ones.** The 1999 National
  Education Act called for compulsory licensing; the 2003 Act created Khurusapha
  and made it mandatory. The statute never carved out an exemption for foreign
  teachers, but it was not written with them in mind either.
- **The waiver as the real route.** For two decades nearly every foreign teacher
  in a Thai government school worked on written permission to teach without a
  license — two years at a time, renewable up to three times. Six years, then the
  waiver is exhausted.
- **What the license pathway actually offers.** The 2023 regulation replaced the
  single five-year license with P, B and A tiers, and a foreigner pathway opened
  in 2024. Two cohorts of 500 have run against a reported waitlist near 10,000 —
  which makes the license a lottery rather than a pathway.
- **The position the law leaves a foreign teacher in.** A foreign national cannot
  hold a Thai teacher civil-service post, so the work is contract staff on
  fixed-term agreements tied to the school year. Even a full A-License does not
  open that door — no tenure, no pension track, and no seat where curriculum is
  decided.
- **The reflection.** The standard itself is right: teaching should be a licensed
  profession. What has not been built out is the capacity behind it, or a status
  the license can lead to.

Researching this changed what "the culture of schooling" means to me. The
staffing pattern in an international-track Thai classroom — who is permanent, who
is renewed annually, who sets the curriculum — is not an accident of any one
school. It is what this law produces.

### Unit 1, Activity 3: Teaching digital citizenship

#### Why teach digital citizenship?

There are a number of reasons to teach digital citizenship: online safety, being
respectful of people with different viewpoints, telling fact from fiction online,
using technology to engage in civil action, balancing time online and off, and
knowing how to stay safe and how to make a space safe for others (ISTE, 2019).

AI belongs in that list now. It is advancing faster every day, and the ways the
general public, students and professionals apply it are still expanding. Because
the technology is moving so fast, our use of it in learning — from both the
student's side and the teacher's — is still nascent. Teachers need to stay on top
of it and start covering it more comprehensively as part of what digital
citizenship means, and as part of what healthy, meaningful learning looks like.

#### Teaching what plagiarism is, and how to avoid it

I would start by defining it and giving concrete examples of what it looks like:

- copying and pasting from a source, online or off, without citing it
- paying someone to do your homework
- using AI to do the work outright, rather than to help you understand it

Then I would teach students how to properly cite, quote and use sources in their
classroom work (MORKSENSEI, 2020). Then — and this is the part that actually
changes behavior — why we would want to avoid it in the first place.

- **Plagiarism hurts the student most of all.** Copying skips the learning: the
  problem solving, the creativity of arriving at your own answer, and the chance
  to make mistakes and learn what mistakes are worth. Mistakes are not a bad
  thing. They are the growth opportunity.
- **It hurts the teacher's read on their own teaching.** Plagiarized work makes it
  impossible to judge whether a concept landed, for that student or for the class.
- **It distorts grading for everyone else.** Plagiarized work is often taken from
  sources well beyond the level of the class, which moves the scale for students
  who did their own.

#### Secondary computer science

**Socially responsible online behavior, and why students need practice.** It means
interacting in digital spaces in ways that respect the dignity, privacy and
wellbeing of others, so that students feel safe voicing their ideas openly. It
includes communicating respectfully in disagreement, protecting personal
information, crediting other people's work, thinking before sharing, and speaking
up — or getting an adult — when witnessing cyberbullying. It is the digital
extension of citizenship: behind every username is a real person, and online
actions have real consequences.

Students need structured opportunities to practice these behaviors, not just hear
about them, because digital citizenship is a skill, not a fact. Practicing in a
supervised space builds habits that transfer to the rest of their digital lives.

**Discord as the safe community platform.** A teacher-administered private server
can be configured for safety: invite-only access, teacher-held moderator
permissions, member-to-member direct messages disabled, and separate channels for
coursework, collaboration and casual community-building. Quieter students can
participate in writing rather than by speaking up in class, and the persistent
chat history lets the teacher monitor interactions and address problems early.

- **Example activity 1 — co-authoring the community guidelines.** Before the server goes
  live, students draft its rules in small groups: what respectful disagreement
  looks like, how to critique a classmate's code without belittling them, what to
  do when someone posts something hurtful. The class negotiates a final version
  and pins it as a living document they agreed to, rather than rules imposed on
  them, which makes the norms a shared responsibility.
- **Example activity 2 — an introductions channel with structured responses.** Once the
  guidelines are pinned, students put them into practice immediately: everyone
  posts a short introduction in `#introductions` — name, an outside interest, and
  something they hope to build this year — following the norms the class just
  agreed on for what is appropriate to share in a semi-public space. Each student
  then replies warmly to at least two classmates they do not know well, with the
  guidelines as the standard for a respectful, welcoming response. Sequencing the
  two this way means students are not writing rules in the abstract; their first
  real interactions on the server are a live application of the community they
  defined, with the teacher modeling it by replying to every post.

**Guiding safe, legal and ethical use of online resources.** I would build three
habits.

- **Attribution.** Code is intellectual property, so copying from Stack Overflow
  without citation is plagiarism. Students cite sources in code comments and use
  properly licensed media.
- **Verification.** Wikipedia is a starting point, not an endpoint. Claims get
  traced to a source and cross-checked.
- **Disclosure.** AI may be used to explain a concept or an error, but all
  assistance must be disclosed, and submitting AI-generated code as your own is
  academic dishonesty. AI can be confidently wrong, so students must be able to
  explain every line they submit — if you cannot explain it, you do not get credit
  for it. And no personal information goes into an AI tool or a public forum.

### Unit 2, Activity 1: Language development and literacy

#### What is language development?

Language development is the process by which a person learns to understand,
speak, read and write a language. It covers vocabulary, grammar, pronunciation,
listening, and the ability to communicate effectively. It starts with sounds and
gestures before it reaches words and sentences, and it carries cognitive, social
and literacy development along with it (Raising Children Network, n.d.). Literacy
is a primary outcome of that development rather than a separate subject bolted on
afterward (Duff & Tomblin, 2018).

#### How does language development impact reading comprehension?

Comprehension depends on it. Students need vocabulary, grammatical knowledge and
oral language before they can make connections across a text, infer what is not
stated, and hold a complex argument together. Where those are strong, reading
comprehension has something to stand on; where they are thin, decoding the words
is not enough.

#### How do differences in languages impact literacy development?

Vocabulary, grammar, sentence structure and writing system all change how a
student learns to read and write. Bilingual children compare their two languages
as they learn, which builds a deeper understanding of both, and they can hold an
advantage over monolingual peers on some reading skills — orthographic,
phonological and semantic skills transfer between languages rather than competing
for space (Mathers, 2020).

#### How do you assess your students' literacy levels?

With more than one measure, because no single one sees the whole picture:

- **Running records**, for reading accuracy, fluency and comprehension.
- **Screening and diagnostic assessments**, for phonics, vocabulary and
  comprehension — screening to find who needs a closer look, diagnostics to find
  what specifically is missing (Foorman et al., 2016).
- **Writing samples**, for writing, spelling, grammar and organization.
- **Classroom observation and student conferences**, for reading behaviors,
  strategy use and engagement — the things a score does not report.

#### How can you differentiate instruction to meet diverse literacy needs?

By adapting content, method and activity to the individual student rather than
lowering the goal (Tomlinson, 2017). In practice: texts at a range of reading
levels; flexible small groups formed from assessment data rather than fixed
ability tiers; vocabulary, phonics and comprehension strategies taught explicitly
instead of left to be absorbed; visual supports, graphic organizers and multimedia
where they carry meaning; and assignments that give more than one way to
demonstrate the same learning.

### Unit 2, Activity 2: Promoting literacy — language development

A twelve-slide presentation arguing that literacy instruction belongs in every
grade and every classroom — secondary computer science included — and proposing a
plan of action a school could actually adopt.

[Download the presentation (.pptx, 62 KB) →](/media/promoting-literacy-language-development.pptx)

#### Literacy is a K–12 responsibility, not an elementary subject

In 2024, 32% of U.S. twelfth graders scored below NAEP Basic in reading — the
highest share on record — and twelfth-grade reading fell three points from 2019 to
the lowest average ever recorded (National Assessment of Educational Progress,
2024). The gaps do not open in high school. Oral language, vocabulary and
comprehension predict reading success years ahead of it, and children who would
later be poor comprehenders showed weak language skills as early as fifteen months
(Shanahan & Lonigan, n.d.). More students are admitted to college than are
prepared for college reading.

That it can be fixed schoolwide is not theoretical. Concourse Village Elementary
in the Bronx — a high-poverty school where 15% of students experience
homelessness — put literacy at the center of every subject: daily collaborative
read-alouds with grade-level and above-grade-level texts, one shared analysis
protocol used everywhere, the same reading and writing strategies carried into
math, science and art, and paired peer observation between teachers. Close to 90%
of students scored proficient or advanced on state math and ELA exams, and the
school earned a National Blue Ribbon (Lexia Learning, n.d.).

#### Computer science is a literacy discipline

Every subject reads and writes in its own way (Boryga, 2022), and CS students read
constantly: technical documentation and specs that punish skimming, source code
and pseudocode where misreading one step breaks every step after it, error
messages and stack traces that have to be decoded, and READMEs and commit messages
written for a real audience (Grafwallner, n.d.). Generic tools bolted on from
elsewhere — a word wall in a lab — fall flat; literacy instruction works when it
is integrated with the content.

What that looks like in the room:

- **Decoding documentation.** Students annotate API docs and assignment specs
  before writing any code, which converts "I don't know what to do" into a
  question they can answer themselves.
- **Direction-word vocabulary.** Highlight the action verbs in every prompt —
  create, review, iterate, return — and keep a class list of the terms that cannot
  be inferred from context. Precision in language is precision in code.
- **Writing to learn.** READMEs, comments and short "how it works" explanations
  for a real reader. Explaining a program in prose exposes fuzzy thinking faster
  than the compiler does.
- **Debugging as close reading.** An error message is a text: what is the claim,
  what is the evidence, what can be inferred. It is the evidence-based reasoning
  of an English class applied to a text the student urgently wants to understand.

#### Recognizing a student who needs support

Margent's seven signs were written about young readers, but they arrive in a high
school CS classroom in translation (Margent, n.d.): "documentation is pointless"
is avoidance masking difficulty; copy-paste coding the student cannot explain is
memorizing words instead of decoding them; finishing step one and asking what to
do next means the written sequence never got processed. Add masking during shared
reading, persistent mix-ups of similar terms — variable and value, argument and
parameter — and rereading the same line harder instead of annotating or chunking.
These are referral flags, not verdicts.

What the research supports for those students is explicit, structured and
cumulative instruction from trained professionals rather than worksheets; *more*
time with real texts rather than less; texts at roughly 98% accuracy alongside
high-interest, low-readability options; and metacognitive work — think-alouds,
annotation, discussion — so students monitor their own comprehension instead of
only decoding (Levin & Porath, n.d.).

#### Plan of action: a four-phase schoolwide initiative

- **Phase 1 — Assess (weeks 1–6).** Screen all students with reading benchmarks,
  have every department audit what its courses actually require students to read
  and write, and identify the students showing warning signs for tiered support.
- **Phase 2 — Embed (quarters 1–2).** Adopt one shared annotation protocol
  schoolwide, and have each department commit to two disciplinary literacy
  routines per unit. Science takes precise vocabulary and accurate lab writing;
  history synthesizes multiple sources into written arguments; math justifies and
  analyzes solutions in mathematical language; computer science annotates specs,
  highlights direction words, writes READMEs and close-reads error messages; world
  languages move from K-W-L to listen–comprehend–speak; ELA anchors the shared
  protocol the other departments apply to their own texts.
- **Phase 3 — Collaborate (ongoing).** A cross-subject literacy team with one
  teacher per department plus ELA and special education runs monthly PLCs and
  keeps grades 9–12 vertically aligned. Intervisitation pairs teachers across
  departments — CS watches ELA teach annotation, ELA watches CS close-read an
  error message. A shared digital literacy framework makes evaluating online
  sources and responsible AI use every course's job rather than one elective's.
- **Phase 4 — Monitor (quarterly).** Mid-year and spring benchmarks track growth,
  data cycles adjust the tiered supports, and student work — blogs, podcasts,
  documentation — is showcased to families each semester.

The technology-rich version of Phase 3 is where my own subject pays back into the
others: CS students build the app or the data visualizer for a partner course and
write the user guide their classmates actually use; students script and publish
podcasts explaining course concepts to a real school audience; peer review runs
through shared docs and version control, the same comment-revise-resubmit cycle
professionals use; and CS and social studies co-teach how search engines,
algorithms and generated text work before students fact-check real sources.

The goal is one sentence: every student reads, writes and discusses
discipline-specific texts every week, in every course, with measurable growth for
the students flagged in Phase 1.
