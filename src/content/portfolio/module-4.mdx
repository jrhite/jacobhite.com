### Unit 1, Activity 1: Building relationships

The first part of my Learning Environment Plan. **Routine strategies** are the
methods I will use every day to build and keep positive relationships with
students. **Intervention strategies** are for the moment one student starts to
drift: they deliberately close the gap between that student and me.

<RelationshipStrategies />

#### Method subject: a student background survey for Secondary Computer Sciences

**Classroom context.** My clinical placement will be a secondary computer
science classroom in an international school in Chiang Mai, Thailand, where
English is the language of instruction and students come from all over the
world: Thailand and the rest of Asia, Europe, North America, Australia, and
beyond. Many students will be multilingual, and their English proficiency will
range from native speakers to students still building academic English. Their
schooling histories will vary just as widely. Some will have come up through
the IB, British, or American systems; others through national systems in their
home countries; and some, often called third culture kids, will have moved
schools and countries several times. Each of those systems treats computer
science differently, so prior coding experience will range from none at all to
years of programming. Access to technology will vary too. Some students will
have their own laptop and fast internet, while others may share a family
computer, rely mainly on a phone, or lose reliable access when they travel home
during breaks. Because coding homework depends on having a computer, I cannot
assume every student has the same access or experience.

**The survey.** It is a Google Form, in English, with short, plain-language
questions so students still building English can answer comfortably.

<StudentSurvey />

Students' self-reported performance in question 4 will be checked against
school records, reviewed with my mentor teacher's permission.

#### Reflection

This survey will help me build cultural competence because it replaces my
assumptions with what students actually tell me. In an international school, it
is easy to group students by nationality, but a student with a Korean passport
may have spent most of her life in Singapore and Dubai. Culturally responsive
teaching asks teachers to use students' own customs, experiences, and
perspectives as tools for instruction (Will & Najarro, 2022), and the survey is
how I learn what those are.

Question 1 is a small example with a large effect: learning to pronounce every
student's name correctly on the first day shows students from every background
that I see them as individuals. Mr. Gomez spent the first minutes of the year
looking for any detail to connect over (SBCUSD, 2018), and the survey gives me
those details for every student, not only the outgoing ones.

Question 7 matters because classroom norms around asking questions differ across
school systems, and Gay's framework emphasizes appreciating different
communication styles rather than treating them as problems (Will & Najarro,
2022). Adolescents are also especially self-conscious about how peers see them
(Pfeifer & Berkman, 2018). If a student says they only ask for help privately,
I will check in quietly at their desk instead of waiting for a raised hand that
may never come.

Question 3 protects relationships as well. If I know a student arrived from a
system with no computer science courses, I will not read their early struggles
as a lack of ability, and they will not experience me as one more adult who
assumed the worst. Each of these is a chance to show students I care before I
ever need to correct them, and as Edutopia (2019) shows, students work harder
for teachers they trust.

#### How I will interpret and use the data

I will organize responses into a simple class profile covering languages and
English comfort, schooling history, technology access, coding experience,
help-seeking preferences, outside commitments, and interests. Then I will act on
each part:

- **Building relationships.** I will use students' interests and app ideas
  (questions 8 and 9) in greetings and one-on-one check-ins, and send each
  family a positive first message early in the year. The U.S. Department of
  Education (2023) recommends consistent, two-way communication with families
  before problems arise.
- **Making instructional decisions.** Coding experience (question 6) will guide
  pair-programming partners and how I differentiate first projects. English
  comfort and schooling history (questions 2 and 3) will tell me where to add
  visual supports, vocabulary previews for terms like *variable* and
  *function*, and worked examples. Interests will shape project themes, so a
  student who plays football might build a match-stats tracker while a student
  who has moved between countries builds a time-zone tool for family calls.
- **Making sure all students are included and engaged.** Technology access
  (question 5) will decide whether homework needs lab time, offline options, or
  phone-friendly versions so no student falls behind because of equipment.
  Outside commitments (question 8) will inform realistic deadlines, and
  help-seeking preferences (question 7) will shape how I run check-ins during
  independent coding time.

I will repeat a shorter version of the survey mid-semester. Adolescents'
identities are still forming and change over time (ACT for Youth, n.d.), new
students arrive throughout the year in international schools, and the data
should change my teaching as my students do.

### Unit 1, Activity 2: Classroom norms

Classroom norms are the foundation for establishing and maintaining a positive
learning environment, and the umbrella that rules sit under. Mine are five
positively stated norms, each with the specific rules that make it concrete,
and each with the modifications it needs in a virtual classroom.

<ClassroomNorms />

#### How these norms lead to a safe learning environment

**Norms and rules work together.** Norms are the familiar, shared ways a class
interacts. They cover broad sets of behavior, apply to everyone in the room (the
teacher included), and are few in number, while rules are specific, observable
statements of what students should do (Developmental Studies Center, n.d.;
Evertson et al., 2003). That is why each of my five norms is listed with the
rules that fit under it. A rule like "Credit any code, images, or AI help you
use" makes the broader norm of using technology responsibly concrete enough to
teach and check. All five norms are stated positively, telling students what to
do rather than what not to do, and the IRIS Center (2021b) notes that positively
stated rules describing expected behavior are linked to less disruptive
behavior.

**How the norms create a safe learning environment.** Safety in my classroom
will have three parts.

- **Emotional safety** comes from norms 1 and 3. My students will come from
  many countries, languages, and school systems, and what counts as respectful
  or engaged differs among them. Dr. Andrew Kwok recommends having students
  define words like *respect* together and allowing more than one way to
  participate (The IRIS Center, 2021b), so we will build what each norm looks
  like as a class instead of assuming one definition.
- **Intellectual safety** comes from norm 2. Programming is a constant cycle of
  errors and fixes, and students who fear looking wrong in front of peers stop
  experimenting. Making bugs a normal, shared part of class lets students take
  the risks that learning to code requires.
- **Physical and digital safety** comes from norms 4 and 5: protecting
  passwords and files, getting permission before sharing anyone's image or
  work, and predictable routines that keep class time focused on learning.

**How I will establish and sustain them.** Because norms belong to the whole
class, I will co-create them with students after the first two to three weeks of
school, once the group has moved past its early forming and storming stages
(Finley, 2014). Students will work in small groups to suggest norms for common
problem areas, and we will vote on the final list and post it. I will then teach
each norm by introducing it, discussing why it matters, modeling examples and
non-examples, practicing, and reviewing it before transitions (The IRIS Center,
2021b). Responding consistently matters as much as the wording, because students
become unsure whether expectations apply to them when teachers enforce them
unevenly (Evertson et al., 2003).

For virtual learning, we will co-construct the same norms on a digital
whiteboard, revisit one norm at the start of each session, and plan for
different devices, connections, and time zones (Rhode Island Department of
Education, n.d.). I will revisit the norms with students mid-semester and adjust
them together. Practiced every day, these norms can shift the classroom climate,
and over time they become part of the class's culture, the way students act
even when no one is checking (Gruenert & Whitaker, 2023).

### Unit 2, Activity 1: Routines and procedures

Step 3 of my Learning Environment Plan: the three routines I will use most in the
classroom, three parallel routines for online classes, and what I will do when
students do not follow them.

#### In-Person Classroom

##### 1. Entry Routine: Log In and Start the Do Now

**Steps**

1. Greet the teacher at the door and walk straight to your assigned computer.
2. Log in with your own school account. If someone else is still signed in, sign them out without opening their files.
3. Open today’s project in Google Classroom and start the Do Now on the board (a short code-reading or debugging puzzle).
4. When the timer ends, be ready to share your answer.


**When students don't follow it**

- Give a quiet, private reminder by pointing to the posted steps, have the student redo only the step that was missed, and thank them when it is done correctly (Kardamis, 2016).
- If several students miss the same step, reteach it to the whole class: introduce, discuss, model, and practice it again (The IRIS Center, 2021a).
- A card at the door lists the steps for late arrivals. With students who are often late, talk privately to understand the reason before deciding how to respond (The IRIS Center, 2021a).
- Keep a loaner laptop and a printed Do Now for login or device problems.


##### 2. Getting Help During Independent Coding

**Steps**

1. Read the error message and try to fix it on your own for 5 minutes.
2. Check class resources (anchor charts, notes, worked examples) or ask a classmate.
3. Add your name to the help queue on the board and keep working on another part of the project.
4. Before the teacher arrives, close any tabs that show personal accounts or messages.


**When students don't follow it**

- Reteach how to read an error message with a worked example.
- Offer a private help channel for students who avoid asking in front of peers.
- For students who ask before trying, use a short “what I tried” checklist before conferencing.
- Provide a glossary of common error messages for English learners.
- If a student pastes a password or API key into their code, show them how to remove it and change the key, then practice the step again.


##### 3. End of Class: Save, Push, and Sign Out

**Steps**

1. At the 5-minute signal, save your work and push your code to GitHub Classroom.
2. Complete the exit ticket (one thing you learned, one question you still have).
3. Sign out of every account (Google, GitHub, coding platform), close the browser, and plug the laptop into the cart.
4. Return to your seat and wait for the teacher to dismiss the class.


**When students don't follow it**

- Give an individual 7-minute warning to students who need more time to transition.
- If a student loses work, show them version history and autosave.
- A rotating “sign-out checker” scans the cart for signed-in screens and closes them without opening anything.
- If a student leaves accounts signed in more than once, practice the sign-out step with them during a break and add a checklist sticker to the laptop.
- Follow up privately with students who repeatedly skip the exit ticket.


#### Parallel Routines for the Online Classroom

##### 1. Entry Routine (Online)

**Steps**

1. Join from the link in Google Classroom 2–3 minutes early and check your camera and microphone.
2. Set your display name to your first or preferred name only, and use a blurred or plain background if your camera is on.
3. Answer the check-in question in the chat, then open the Do Now link pinned in the chat.
4. Mute your microphone until the teacher invites you to speak.


**When students don't follow it**

- Students with weak connections may join by audio only or complete the check-in through a short form.
- Late joiners read the pinned chat to catch up. Students in another time zone complete an asynchronous version of the Do Now.
- If a display name shows a full name or personal details, rename it privately with host controls and send a reminder message.
- Address repeated issues in a private message, not in front of the class.


##### 2. Getting Help During Online Coding

**Steps**

1. Read the error message and try to fix it on your own for 5 minutes.
2. Check the class question page in Google Classroom. If your question is not there, post it with your code snippet.
3. Use the raise-hand tool or add your name to the shared help queue.
4. Before sharing your screen, close personal tabs, email, and notifications.


**When students don't follow it**

- Offer private direct messages and short virtual office hours for students who will not ask in front of others.
- Post short screen-capture videos of common fixes.
- Reteach how to share a screen or paste code for students new to the platform.
- If private information appears on a shared screen or in the chat, stop the share, remove the message, and talk with the student privately. Use it as a teachable moment: chats are saved, so what is posted online lasts longer than something said in the room (Walton, 2020).


##### 3. End of an Online Class

**Steps**

1. Push your code and submit the assignment link in Google Classroom.
2. Complete the exit ticket form posted in the chat.
3. Listen as the teacher summarizes next steps and where to find the recording.
4. Leave the call when dismissed and sign out of class tools, especially on a shared family device.


**When students don't follow it**

- If a connection drops before submission, students may submit by the end of the day without penalty.
- Send an automatic reminder through Google Classroom to students who have not submitted.
- Give families who share a device a short sign-out checklist.
- Follow up individually with students who leave early more than once.



#### Computer Science Methods: Personal Data, Digital Identity, and Privacy

##### Routine: Monday Privacy Think-Aloud (the teacher models managing data)

**What happens**

1. I project a short, fictional example (an app’s permission screen, a sample public profile, or a code file with a password left in it) and think aloud about what I check and why.
2. In pairs, students use our privacy checklist to find the risks and suggest a fix.
3. We agree on one fix as a class, and I show how to make it on a demo account.
4. I post the example and checklist in Google Classroom so students can review them later.


Online: I share my screen, students mark risks on a shared slide, and the captioned recording is posted afterward.

**When students don't follow it**

- Students who miss the lesson watch the captioned recording and complete the checklist.
- Students who prefer not to speak in front of the class can post answers in the chat or on a sticky note.
- Check in privately with students who seem disengaged and give them a role, such as checklist reader.


##### Routine: Friday Data Check (students practice managing their personal data)

**What happens**

1. Open your data log (paper or digital, your choice) and list any new apps or sites that now hold your data.
2. Check one privacy setting from this week’s card (for example, GitHub profile visibility, app permissions, or location sharing).
3. Strengthen one password or turn on two-step verification where it is offered.
4. Write one sentence about what you changed and the tradeoff between convenience and security.


Online: The same four steps run as a 5-minute form at the start of Friday’s session. Any screenshots must crop out personal details.

**When students don't follow it**

- Students without personal accounts or devices practice on a sample account I provide.
- Students who skip the check complete it during the next Do Now.
- Pair unsure students with a “privacy buddy” who explains settings without ever seeing passwords.
- Provide a short glossary and a one-on-one walkthrough for students who need more support.


##### Procedures and expectations: personal data and digital identity

**What happens**

1. Use your school account for class tools. Ask before signing up for any new tool, and never use a personal email for class.
2. Public work (GitHub repositories, project demos) shows only your first name or a chosen username: no full name, birthday, address, location, or photos of faces.
3. Ask permission before posting a classmate’s name, image, voice, or code, and credit other people’s work.
4. Before posting, ask: “Would I be comfortable if a university, employer, or family member saw this in five years?”


Online: The same expectations apply, plus no recording or screenshots of classmates during calls.

**When students don't follow it**

- If a student posts personal information, help them remove it privately, including from the commit history, since Git keeps old versions.
- Hold a short restorative conversation when a post affects a classmate.
- Reteach the expectation by introducing, modeling, and practicing it again, and contact the family if it happens again.


##### Procedure: Privacy by design in student coding projects

**What happens**

1. Use made-up sample data. Never use a classmate’s or family member’s real name, photo, or contact details.
2. Keep passwords and API keys in a secrets file listed in .gitignore, never in the code you push.
3. If your program collects information from users, collect only what it needs and show a short notice saying what is collected and why.
4. Before publishing, have a partner review the project with the privacy checklist.


Online: The same steps apply, and partner reviews happen through GitHub comments.

**When students don't follow it**

- Provide a starter template with the .gitignore file and sample data already set up.
- If a key is pushed by mistake, help the student remove it from the history and change it, with no penalty the first time.
- Offer a shorter checklist for students who need it.


##### Procedure: Suspicious messages, AI tools, and account problems

**What happens**

1. Do not type personal information (yours or anyone else’s) into AI chatbots or sites the class has not approved.
2. If a message asks for your password or pushes you to click a link, do not respond. Report it with the class “Report it” form or tell the teacher.
3. If you think someone else used your account, tell the teacher right away. We change the password and turn on two-step verification together, with no blame.
4. Review the account’s recent sign-in activity together to see what happened.


Online: The report form is pinned in Google Classroom, and students can message me privately.

**When students don't follow it**

- Students can report anonymously.
- Show examples of scam messages in several languages, since they reach students in every language.
- A student who clicks a scam link gets a private follow-up, never public correction.


##### Teacher procedures to ensure the privacy of student data

**What happens**

1. Use only tools the school has approved, including free click-through apps, after checking what data they collect. Share the list of class tools with families in plain language, translated when needed.
2. Collect as little as possible: class repositories are private by default, grades and feedback stay in the learning management system, and real student data is never used in demo code or slides.
3. Give each student their login details privately, lock my screen when I step away, keep student data off personal devices, and delete it at the end of the year according to school policy.
4. Report any exposure of student data to school leadership the same day, and follow the school’s policy for informing families.


Online: Unique meeting links and a waiting room admit only school accounts. Recordings are announced, stored in the school drive, shared only with the class, and deleted at the end of the term.

**When students don't follow it**

- If a student wants to use an unapproved app, offer an approved alternative or ask the school to review it.
- If a student shares a login, reset it right away and reteach why accounts are personal.
- If a student uses a classmate’s account, address it privately and connect it to our norm on protecting passwords.


##### Designed for every student

- Every routine can be completed on a school device and school account, so no student needs a personal phone, email, or home internet. Paper versions of the data log and checklists are always available.
- Checklists and setting cards use plain English, icons, and screenshots, work with screen readers and translation tools, and come with captioned videos.
- Cameras are optional online, and students choose their usernames and what they share about themselves, since expectations about privacy differ across families and cultures.
- Examples include apps and situations from many countries, not only one region.
- Students who need it get extra time, a shorter checklist, or a one-on-one walkthrough.


#### How these routines support every learner

**A safe, caring, respectful, and productive environment for students with exceptionalities.** My routines are designed so students with exceptionalities, including students with disabilities, students with attention or anxiety needs, English learners, and advanced learners, know what to do and can succeed without being singled out. Each procedure is posted as a short numbered list with icons, so students who struggle with working memory or reading in English can check the next step on their own instead of waiting for help, and every routine can be completed on a school device and account. Safety comes from privacy: the private help channel, the “what I tried” checklist, optional cameras online, and private corrections let students with anxiety or learning differences ask for help and make mistakes without public embarrassment. Respect and care come from looking for the reason behind a missed routine before responding, especially when a disability or family circumstance affects something like arriving on time (The IRIS Center, 2021a). Productivity comes from building supports into the routines themselves, such as the 7-minute transition warning, starter templates, shorter checklists, and one-on-one walkthroughs, and from following each student’s individual support plan (such as an IEP) within every routine. Advanced learners extend the same routines by serving as privacy buddies or sign-out checkers.

**How expectations are taught, reinforced, and maintained.** I teach expectations explicitly during the first two weeks. For each routine, I introduce the steps, discuss why they matter, model examples and non-examples, have students practice before the routine is needed, and review it often (Cox, 2019; The IRIS Center, 2021a). Students with exceptionalities get extra rehearsal, a visual copy of the steps at their desk, and, when helpful, a private preview before the class practices. I reinforce expectations with behavior-specific praise tied to the step a student completed (The IRIS Center, 2021a), and when a step is missed, I kindly correct it and have the student redo only that part (Kardamis, 2016). To maintain expectations, I respond the same way across students and over time (Evertson et al., 2003), reteach a routine whenever several students struggle with it or after a long break, and review how each routine is working during the year to decide where to add or remove structure (Edutopia, 2022).

**Predictability, engagement, and positive interactions.** Every lesson opens and closes the same way, the online class follows the same steps, and class information lives in one place, so students always know what comes next and where to look (Zimny, 2020). Classrooms with predictable procedures have lower rates of challenging behavior, and explicitly taught procedures help students monitor their own behavior (The IRIS Center, 2021a). This predictability especially helps students with autism, ADHD, or anxiety, for whom unexpected change can be a barrier to learning. The routines also keep students engaged: the Do Now starts learning in the first minute, the help queue keeps students coding while they wait, and the exit ticket gives every student a voice at the end of class. Finally, they build positive interactions. The greeting at the door and the online check-in start each class with personal contact, privacy buddies and pair roles give students structured ways to help each other, and caring routines carried into the online classroom help every student feel welcome and valued (Zimny, 2020).

#### Why these routines and procedures

**Choosing the routines.** The IRIS Center (2021a) advises keeping procedures simple and building them for the less structured parts of class, where problems are most likely, and suggests asking why, where, what, who, when, and how for each one. In a computer lab, those moments are entering, getting help while coding, and packing up, when time and devices are most easily lost. The same three appear on the Milwaukee Public Schools PBIS (2014) planning form for classroom procedures. Edutopia (2022) describes the cycle I will follow: identify where a routine is needed, build in time to demonstrate and practice it during the first weeks, then assess it over time and decide where to add or remove structure.

**Teaching them and responding when students don’t follow them.** I will teach each routine by explaining it, practicing it, and reinforcing it until it becomes automatic (Cox, 2019), following the IRIS Center’s (2021a) sequence of introduce, discuss, model, practice, and review, and posting each one as a numbered list. Correction is part of teaching. Kardamis (2016) recommends kindly correcting the part done wrong, having students redo just that part, and praising them when they get it right, which is the first response in my modifications column. I will pair this with behavior-specific praise (The IRIS Center, 2021a) and respond the same way every time, since uneven enforcement leaves students unsure what is expected (Evertson et al., 2003). When a student keeps missing a routine, I will look for the reason first. Andrew Kwok explains that lateness and similar behaviors often have causes outside a student’s control, and that understanding them makes classroom management more culturally responsive (The IRIS Center, 2021a), which matters in an international school where students arrive with different expectations about school.

**Online parallels.** The online routines keep the same steps so students do not have to relearn them when they move between settings. Zimny (2020) recommends using the same few routines repeatedly, keeping information in one place, and creating a shared question page that students check before asking. Walton (2020) adds that teachers cannot assume students know how to learn online, so routines should be modeled and practiced, login details shared privately, and students reminded to sign out to protect their privacy. Allowances for different devices, connections, and time zones keep the routines fair for every student (Rhode Island Department of Education, n.d.).

**Personal data, digital identity, and privacy.** In a computer science class, protecting data is both content and routine. The ISTE Standards for Students ask students to manage their digital identity and protect their privacy and personal data online (International Society for Technology in Education, 2024). The CSTA standards ask secondary students to describe tradeoffs between keeping information public and private (2-IC-23), explain privacy concerns in automated data collection (3A-IC-29), weigh the implications of privacy for safety, law, and ethics (3A-IC-30), and recommend and compare security measures (3A-NI-06, 3A-NI-07; Computer Science Teachers Association, 2017). I model these skills before asking students to practice them. The Monday think-aloud follows the same introduce, model, and practice sequence I use for every procedure (The IRIS Center, 2021a), the Friday Data Check turns that practice into a weekly habit, and the privacy-by-design steps apply the same habits to the programs students write. The digital identity expectations build on Common Sense Education’s (n.d.) focus on how online actions last and reach unseen audiences. My own procedures follow the Privacy Technical Assistance Center’s (2014) advice to know which online services are in use, review even free click-through tools before adopting them, and be open with families about how student information is used. Every routine can be completed on a school device and account, uses plain-language materials that can be translated, and leaves students in control of what they share. This way, students without home technology, English learners, students with disabilities, and students whose families hold different expectations about privacy can all take part fully (Rhode Island Department of Education, n.d.; Will & Najarro, 2022).

### Unit 3, Activity 1: Classroom arrangements

#### Three seating arrangements and when I would use them

As a future computer science teacher, the arrangement I expect to use most is pair
pods. Pair programming is a core practice in CS. One student "drives" at the
keyboard while the other "navigates," and they swap roles on a timer. Pair pods
make that routine natural. Because I choose the pairs, I can match students on
purpose. For example, I can pair a confident coder with a student who is still
building fluency, so both have a chance to contribute (Davies, 2025). A pair is
also a manageable unit. Darren Thomas notes that larger groups bring a lot of
social energy but are the hardest to monitor, and students easily drift off task
(Educational Research Techniques, 2022). Two students still get the benefits of
collaboration and talking through problems without a table full of distractions.
The pods also leave aisles open, so I can circulate, look at screens, and help
debug (Davies, 2025). Pairs also work well for quick partner checks, like asking
each column of pairs to trace a different piece of code before we compare answers
(Renard, 2020). One drawback is that some students may need to sit alone for a
while to stay focused. When that happens, I would explain the reason and what it
takes to rejoin a partner, so the move never feels like a punishment (Davies,
2025).

Next, I would use the horseshoe or semicircle for discussion-heavy lessons. In CS,
those include debating the ethics of AI, reviewing a classmate's code, or watching
a live demo on the projector. Everyone can see the board and also make eye contact
with each other, which supports real discussion between students and the teacher
(Poorvu Center for Teaching and Learning, n.d.). Thomas calls the half circle "the
best of both worlds." The teacher keeps control of the discussion from the open end
of the U, and students can still interact with one another (Educational Research
Techniques, 2022). The Poorvu Center points out that students on the sides of the U
can get less attention, so I would make a point of calling on them (Poorvu Center
for Teaching and Learning, n.d.). The horseshoe takes a lot of floor space, and it
doesn't work well for small-group work (Renard, 2020). For bigger classes, I would
switch to a double horseshoe.

Lastly, I would use traditional rows when the task is individual. Examples include
quizzes, coding assessments, and short direct-instruction segments where all eyes
need to be on the front. Rows reduce side conversations and give every student a
clear view of the board (Davies, 2025). However, rows limit eye contact and
discussion, and students can get bored when rows become the default (Educational
Research Techniques, 2022). Students in the back rows also tend to be less engaged
(Poorvu Center for Teaching and Learning, n.d.). So I would treat rows as a short
phase of the lesson rather than the whole lesson. Thomas suggests starting class in
rows to give instructions and then breaking students into another arrangement for
the task (Educational Research Techniques, 2022). When I can't move the furniture,
I can still add interaction with Think-Pair-Share between neighbors (Poorvu Center
for Teaching and Learning, n.d.).

For all three arrangements, I see seating as a tool I can change. A layout that
works in the first month may need to change later, and changing it is a normal part
of classroom management, not a failure (Davies, 2025). This fits InTASC Standard 3,
because I would match the room to the kind of learning each lesson requires, whether
that is collaborative, discussion-based, or individual.

#### In-person transitions

##### 1. Entering the Classroom

**Steps**

1. Greet the teacher at the door and check the agenda posted on the screen.
2. Go straight to your assigned computer and log in with your own school account.
3. Open today's Do Now from the pinned post in Google Classroom.
4. Start the Do Now before the 3-minute timer ends. The teacher takes attendance from it.


**Supports:** Agenda shown with icons as well as words. Do Now written in short, plain-English sentences with key terms glossed, and a printed copy at the desk for students who need it. Students with anxiety or sensory needs may enter a minute early to settle before the room fills.

##### 2. Whole Group to Small Group

**Steps**

1. Give the attention signal and start a 2-minute countdown on the screen.
2. Post group lists and roles (driver, navigator, reporter, timekeeper) on the board.
3. One student repeats the steps aloud before the teacher says "Move."
4. Students save their work and move to their assigned table with only their laptops.
5. Groups confirm roles and begin the posted task before the timer ends.


**Supports:** Groups and roles are shown with names and icons and also read aloud. Role cards include sentence starters ("I think we should try..."). Groups are planned so a student still building English has a peer who can clarify in a shared language.

##### 3. Small Group to Whole Group

**Steps**

1. Give a 1-minute warning, then the attention signal (chime and "laptops at 45").
2. Students finish their sentence, save their work, and half-close their laptops.
3. Students return to their own seats.
4. Each group's reporter gets ready to share one finding.


**Supports:** A visible countdown helps students who find it hard to stop an absorbing task (Finley, 2017). Reporters may read from a sentence frame ("Our group found..."). A student with mobility needs can stay where they are while others move.

##### 4. Direct Instruction to Independent Coding (technology)

**Steps**

1. Post the task and success criteria, with screenshots, on the screen and in Google Classroom.
2. Check understanding with a quick thumbs up, sideways, or down.
3. Students open the starter code from Google Classroom and begin.
4. The teacher checks in first with students who signaled sideways or down.


**Supports:** Starter code includes commented hints that break the task into smaller steps. Written instructions work with translation tools and screen readers. Students with an IEP or 504 plan receive their chunked checklist or extra time as written in the plan.

##### 5. "Lids Down": Coding to Unplugged Discussion (technology)

**Steps**

1. Give the "lids down" signal with a 30-second countdown.
2. Students save their work and close their laptops halfway.
3. Students turn their chairs toward the speaker or discussion circle.
4. Discussion begins once every screen is down.


**Supports:** Students who hyperfocus on screens (common with ADHD or autism) get a quiet, private 2-minute heads-up first. Discussion questions are posted in writing, and a short think-pair-share lets English learners rehearse an answer before speaking to the whole class.

##### 6. Independent Coding to Pair Programming (technology)

**Steps**

1. Post partner pairs on the screen (planned using survey and progress data).
2. Pairs sit at one computer: the driver at the keyboard, the navigator beside them.
3. Start the visible role-swap timer (every 10 minutes).
4. Pairs read the task together and agree on a plan before writing code.


**Supports:** Partners are matched by coding experience and language background. Role cards list key phrases ("Can you scroll up?", "Let's test it") with space for home-language translations. Students who struggle with fine motor control can begin as navigator.

##### 7. Restroom and Water Breaks

**Steps**

1. Hold up the agreed hand signal and wait for the teacher's nod.
2. Save your work and lock your screen.
3. Sign out on the digital hall pass, take the physical pass, and leave quietly. One student goes at a time.
4. Return quietly, sign back in on the hall pass, unlock your screen, and check the board for what changed.


**Supports:** The hand signal means no student has to ask aloud, in English, in front of the class (Finley, 2017). Students whose IEP, 504, or health plan allows it leave without signaling. The pass has a picture as well as a word, and breaks are never discussed in front of peers.

##### 8. Leaving for and Returning from Pull-Out Support (EAL or Learning Support)

**Steps**

1. A timer alert reminds students 2 minutes before their scheduled support session.
2. Students save their work, take their materials, and leave quietly without a class announcement.
3. On return, students check the "While You Were Out" post in Google Classroom.
4. A designated buddy gives a 1-minute catch-up, and the student joins the current task.


**Supports:** The "While You Were Out" post uses short sentences and screenshots so it can be read quickly or translated. The buddy is a classmate the student is comfortable with, and the leaving is never announced, so no student is singled out.

##### 9. Switching to Plan B When the Network or a Tool Goes Down (technology)

**Steps**

1. The teacher says "Plan B" and shows the Plan B slide.
2. Students save locally or take a screenshot of their code, then close their laptops.
3. Table captains collect the printed Plan B packet (pseudocode, trace tables, or a debugging puzzle on paper).
4. Students work in their current pairs until the teacher signals that devices are back.


**Supports:** Plan B packets follow the same layout as online tasks, so the switch is familiar. Large-print and simplified versions are in the packet folder. Calm, predictable steps help students for whom an unexpected change is stressful.

##### 10. Getting Out and Returning Shared Equipment (robotics kits, microcontrollers, headphones)

**Steps**

1. Each group's materials manager collects the group's numbered kit.
2. The group checks the contents against the photo checklist on the lid.
3. At the 5-minute signal, students unplug, pack, and repeat the checklist.
4. The materials manager returns the kit to its numbered shelf before the timer ends.


**Supports:** Photo checklists mean the kit can be checked without reading English. The materials manager role rotates and is a good first leadership role for a student who struggles with transitions (Brukner, 2021). Students with fine motor needs receive pre-sorted kits.

##### 11. Fire Drill or Emergency Evacuation

**Steps**

1. At the alarm, stop, close laptops, and leave everything on the desk.
2. Line up at the door in the posted order, with your buddy.
3. Walk silently to the assembly point and stand with the class for the roll call.
4. On return, log back in and check the pinned post for where we left off.


**Supports:** Drills are practiced in the first week with a visual route map. Students with sensory sensitivities are warned before scheduled drills and may wear headphones. Newer students and English learners are paired with a buddy, and students with mobility needs follow their individual evacuation plan.

##### 12. Exiting the Classroom

**Steps**

1. At the 5-minute signal, save your work and push your code to GitHub Classroom.
2. Complete the exit ticket.
3. Sign out of every account, close the browser, and plug the laptop into the cart.
4. Return to your seat and wait for the teacher to dismiss the class.


**Supports:** Students who need more time get a private 7-minute warning. The exit ticket can be answered with a sentence frame or a quick drawing or code snippet. A checklist sticker on each laptop shows the sign-out steps with icons.

#### Online transitions

##### 1. Entering the Online Class

**Steps**

1. Join from the Google Classroom link 2–3 minutes early and wait in the waiting room.
2. Check your camera and microphone, and set your display name to your preferred first name.
3. Answer the check-in question in the chat.
4. Open the Do Now link pinned in the chat and begin.


**Supports:** Live captions are turned on for the whole session. Students with weak connections may join by audio only and answer the check-in with a short form. The Do Now link is posted in the chat and in Google Classroom, so students who join late can find it.

##### 2. Whole Group to Breakout Rooms

**Steps**

1. Post the task, roles, and time limit in the chat and on a shared slide before opening rooms.
2. Open breakout rooms with a visible countdown timer.
3. Groups assign roles and open the shared document or editor linked in the chat.
4. The teacher visits each room within the first 3 minutes.


**Supports:** Instructions are pinned in writing because spoken directions are easy to miss online. English learners are placed with a peer who can clarify in a shared language. Students who find breakout rooms stressful may choose the "chat-only" reporter role.

##### 3. Breakout Rooms to Main Room

**Steps**

1. Broadcast a 2-minute warning to all rooms.
2. Groups save their work to the shared class folder.
3. Rooms close automatically and students return to the main room.
4. Reporters use the raise-hand tool and unmute one at a time to share.


**Supports:** Reporters may type their summary in the chat instead of speaking. The shared folder means no student loses work if their connection drops during the move.

##### 4. Direct Instruction to Independent Coding (technology)

**Steps**

1. The teacher stops screen sharing and posts the task link and success criteria in the chat.
2. Students react with a thumbs-up once their editor is open.
3. Students code with microphones muted and cameras optional.
4. The teacher stays in the main room to answer the help queue.


**Supports:** A short captioned screen recording of the demo is posted so students can rewatch it at their own pace. Students can ask for help privately by direct message.

##### 5. Coding to Whole-Class Discussion

**Steps**

1. Post a 1-minute warning in the chat.
2. Students save their work and close other tabs.
3. Students turn cameras on (per our class agreement) and focus on the shared screen.
4. Students take turns using the raise-hand tool or the chat.


**Supports:** Students can answer aloud, in the chat, or on an anonymous Pear Deck slide. Questions stay on screen in writing, and students get 30 seconds of think time before anyone answers.

##### 6. Independent Coding to Online Pair Programming (technology)

**Steps**

1. Post partner pairs and breakout room numbers in the chat.
2. The driver opens the shared live-coding editor so both partners can see and edit the code.
3. Pairs set a role-swap timer.
4. Pairs post their plan in one chat message before they start coding.


**Supports:** Partners are matched by experience and language background. The shared editor's comments let partners communicate in writing as well as speech, which helps students still building spoken English.

##### 7. Switching Between Online Tools (slides, Pear Deck, code editor) (technology)

**Steps**

1. The teacher names the next tool and posts its link in the chat.
2. Students open the link and react with a thumbs-up when it has loaded.
3. Students close the previous tab so only class tools are open.
4. The activity starts once most students have reacted. The teacher helps anyone still loading by direct message.


**Supports:** All links also sit on one class hub page in the same order as the agenda, so a student who falls behind can always find the current activity. Changing tools every 10 to 15 minutes keeps the session active for students who struggle to sustain attention in front of a screen.

##### 8. Short Breaks and Stepping Away Online

**Steps**

1. Post "brb" in the chat or use the "away" reaction.
2. Turn your camera off and mute your microphone.
3. When you return, post "back" and check the pinned chat for the current task.


**Supports:** No one has to explain why they are stepping away. The teacher builds a whole-class movement break into every 25 minutes of screen time, which helps students with attention needs (Brukner, 2021).

##### 9. Rejoining After a Dropped Connection or Late Arrival

**Steps**

1. Rejoin with the class link without interrupting. The teacher will check in.
2. Read the pinned chat for the current task and time stamps.
3. Message your connection buddy privately for a quick catch-up.
4. If you cannot rejoin, watch the recording and complete the asynchronous version by the posted deadline.


**Supports:** Students who rely on mobile data or share a device are never penalized for a dropped connection. The recording has captions, and the asynchronous version uses the same steps as the live lesson.

##### 10. Leaving the Online Class

**Steps**

1. Push your code and submit the assignment link in Google Classroom.
2. Complete the exit ticket form posted in the chat.
3. Listen as the teacher summarizes next steps and where to find the recording.
4. Leave the call when dismissed and sign out of class tools, especially on a shared family device.


**Supports:** The next steps are posted in writing as well as spoken. Families who share a device receive a short sign-out checklist with icons.

#### How the plan holds together

**Keeping the online class live and interactive.** Online lessons change activity every 10 to 15 minutes, moving between a short demo, a Pear Deck question everyone answers, pair coding in breakout rooms, and a quick share-out in the main room. Every change follows the same pattern: a warning in the chat, one link to open, a thumbs-up when ready, and a visible timer. Students always know what is happening next, and the changes of activity give them regular chances to speak, type, vote, and code instead of watching a screen for a full period.

**Supporting students with learning differences and language barriers.** Students may struggle with transitions because of sensory processing, emotional regulation, attention, or speech and language processing, and they benefit from routine, structure, and predictability (Brukner, 2021). Four supports run through every transition above. Predictability: every transition uses the same signal, a visible timer, and the same steps every time. Advance notice: students who find it hard to stop an activity, including many students with ADHD or autism, get a private heads-up before the class warning, and accommodations in IEPs and 504 plans are followed in every transition. Language support: directions are given aloud and in writing, in short sentences with icons, gestures, and hand signals. Key transition words (save, push, log out, lids down) are on the word wall with home-language translations, students can use a bilingual buddy, and online sessions use live captions and posted links that work with translation tools. Regulation and ownership: after an energetic activity the class takes one collective deep breath, students who need them can use quiet fidgets, and students who struggle most with transitions are given leadership roles such as timekeeper or materials manager, which builds their ownership and confidence (Brukner, 2021).

**Why these transitions.** Secondary computer science students move between whole-class, small-group, partner, independent, and unplugged work several times in one lesson, and every transition is a chance to lose learning time. Finley (2017) recommends getting attention, explaining the procedure, preparing students for a start signal, and then watching that students follow it, along with countdowns, visible timers, hand signals for requests such as restroom breaks, and precorrection, where a student describes the steps before the class moves. Each transition above follows that pattern. The EngageNY (2015) video shows a teacher guiding students quietly back into small groups with little loss of instructional time, which is the standard I am aiming for: short, calm transitions directed with clear cues instead of raised voices. I will teach and practice each transition during the first two weeks and have the class redo any that run slowly, as Finley (2017) suggests, because predictable routines let students spend more of the period learning (Evertson et al., 2003). The technology transitions (moving into coding, "lids down", pair programming, switching tools, Plan B, and shared equipment) address the moments when screens and devices most often pull attention away from learning in a computer science classroom.

**Written online supports.** Live captions, pinned instructions, and clickable links that work with translation tools.

#### Method subject: the blank file is a canvas

Programming can look like the opposite of creative work, all exact syntax and
right answers. But every program starts as something that does not exist yet,
and someone has to imagine it. Creativity also depends on climate. School climate
includes the safety and relationships people feel every day (Strobel, 2023), and
a student who does not feel safe being wrong will not try an idea that might
fail.

#### Strategies to nurture creativity and creative expression

- **Let students choose the problem.** Each student will build one real
  application across the term, starting from something that actually annoys
  someone they know. Resnick (2017) names projects, passion, peers, and play as
  the conditions for creative learning, and a project a student cares about
  supplies the first two.
- **Keep the concept fixed and the product open.** Everyone has to use lists and
  loops this week, but one student might build a match-stats tracker and another
  a time-zone tool for calling family abroad.
- **Make time for play and peers.** Short "what happens if I change this?"
  sessions come before any correct solution, and students show work in progress
  to a partner while it can still change.
- **Treat bugs as ordinary.** My classroom norms already do this, because a
  student who fears a failed attempt stops experimenting.

#### Two ways I can model creativity

1. **Live-code a problem I have not solved.** I will think out loud, try an idea,
   hit a bug, throw the idea away, and try another. Students see that the first
   attempt is rarely the final one, and that an engineer with decades of
   experience still works this way.
2. **Show my own unfinished work.** I will share a real project of mine, including
   the sketches, the rejected versions, and the suggestions from an AI tool that I
   turned down, and explain why. Students see that the idea and the decision stay
   with the person, even when AI writes some of the code.

#### How my modeling shapes what students try

People learn a great deal by watching others (Bandura, 1977), so what I do with my
own mistakes teaches more than anything I say about them. If students only ever
see finished code, they conclude that good programmers do not fail, and they
start to copy rather than invent. When I throw away my own idea in front of them,
a failed attempt becomes part of the work, and trying something unusual feels
safe. So each thing I model comes with a move for students:

- **I show a failed attempt, and students keep an "idea graveyard."** Each project
  includes a file of ideas they tried and dropped, and at each demo they share one
  favorite failure and what it taught them.
- **I show the AI suggestions I rejected, and students do the same.** They annotate
  a suggestion from an AI tool, marking what they kept, changed, or threw away, and
  why.
- **I ask "what if?" out loud, and students ask it of each other.** During partner
  reviews, every student offers one "what if?" about a classmate's project.

I will know the modeling is working when project ideas get stranger, the graveyard
files get longer, and "is this right?" gives way to "what if I tried this?"

#### Using creativity to communicate ideas, knowledge, and connections

- **Ideas.** Students will demo what they built and explain it in their own
  words. A working demo, a diagram, or a storyboard also gives students still
  building academic English a way to show complex thinking. The ISTE student
  standards expect learners to express themselves creatively with digital tools
  (International Society for Technology in Education, 2024).
- **Knowledge.** Students can write a programming joke. In an online math lesson I
  observed, the class used a joke that plays on two meanings of *right* and
  *obtuse* (Moreland University, 2021). A joke about an off-by-one error only
  works if its writer understands the error.
- **Connections among concepts.** Students will draw how data moves through their
  app from the input to the database and back, then invent their own analogy for
  it. That shows me how they connect variables, functions, and storage better than
  a list of definitions would.

### Unit 4, Activity 2: Creating a positive, information-intensive environment

#### Vlog: information-intensive environments and oral language

Hi, I'm Jacob Hite, and I'm preparing to teach secondary computer science. When students walk into my classroom, I want them surrounded by language: a word wall, charts, books, code on the walls, and people talking about all of it. That is an information-intensive environment, and The Access Center (2007) explains that this kind of setting draws students into language and literacy every day, so they see what spoken and written language are for.

How does it build vocabulary? Through repeated, meaningful contact. A word like "variable" appears on the word wall with a definition, an icon, and a code example. It appears in a beginner's guide for a seventh grader and in official documentation for a twelfth grader. Those levels matter. Developmentally appropriate print and digital texts mean every student can reach the same idea. The National Children's Book and Literacy Alliance (2015) also recommends reading aloud daily, a little above grade level, so students hear new words used well. Students add home-language terms too, because print in a student's first language supports multicultural learners (The Access Center, 2007).

This shapes both climate and culture. Climate is the safety and support students feel each day (Strobel, 2023). A student who has the right words can ask for help without embarrassment, and a student who finds the answer on the wall gains independence. Culture is what we value (Strobel, 2023). Pratiwi et al. (2019) describe displaying student work and a storytelling chair in a kindergarten. I would adapt these for teenagers with a code gallery and a demo chair. Over time, "let's look it up" and "show us what you built" become how we do things here.

Oral language sits underneath all of this. Students hear and say words before they read and write them well. Mometrix Academy (2022) describes literacy-rich content classrooms where students listen to several viewpoints and support their points with evidence. In pair programming, students say out loud what the code does. That spoken explanation becomes a written comment, and later a project README. Talk rehearses writing, and reading gives students more language for talk.

Finally, in computer science, much of what students read is online, so curiosity needs critical thinking. We keep a curiosity board for real questions. Students use SIFT to check sources (Caulfield, 2019), read sideways like fact-checkers (Wineburg & McGrew, 2019), and treat AI output as a claim to test. They can do this in any language, and with sentence frames for support, so every student takes part. Thanks for watching.

### Step 5 of my Learning Environment Plan

#### Steps to create the environment

1. **Computing word wall.** Key terms (variable, loop, conditional, function, debug) each have a plain-English definition, an icon, and a short code example. Students can add the term in their home languages next to the English.
2. **Anchor charts at eye level.** How to read an error message, our help routine, pair programming roles, and our class norms, so students can find answers without waiting for me.
3. **Classroom library of print and digital texts.** Beginner coding guides at different reading levels, graphic novels and biographies of computing pioneers from many countries, and short tech news articles, with digital copies linked in Google Classroom.
4. **Rotating student work gallery.** Printed, commented code snippets and project screenshots with QR codes that open the working program.
5. **Digital resource hub.** A Google Classroom page with language documentation, captioned tutorial videos, worked examples, and an error-message glossary, available in class, at home, and during online learning.
6. **Levels of text for grades 7–12.** Younger students start with illustrated guides and short tutorials. Older students move to official documentation and technical articles. I will rotate the texts by project and by student interest, and include both informational and literary choices (The Access Center, 2007; Just Right Reader, n.d.).
7. **Daily oral language.** Each lesson includes a two-minute read-aloud of code or a short article, and I think aloud as I read. Pairs say what the code does before they write a comment, so spoken explanations become written ones (The Access Center, 2007; National Children's Book and Literacy Alliance, 2015). Online, this happens in breakout rooms or short voice recordings.
8. **Places to share and be heard.** Pratiwi et al. (2019) describe message centers, displays of student work, and a storytelling chair in a kindergarten. I will adapt these for secondary students as a class question board, the gallery above, and a "demo chair" where a student presents a program to the class.

#### How it will affect classroom climate

An information-rich room tells students that help is always available and that they are expected to use it. When a student is stuck on an error, the anchor chart and glossary let them take the first step on their own, which builds independence and lowers the anxiety of asking in front of peers. The IRIS Center (2021b) notes that posting expectations where everyone can see them lets teachers redirect quickly and positively. Representation matters too: when the library and word wall reflect computing pioneers and languages from around the world, students in an international school can see themselves in the field, which culturally responsive teaching identifies as key to belonging and engagement (Will & Najarro, 2022). Displaying student code makes their work part of the room, so the space feels like it belongs to everyone (Williams, 2024). Captioned videos and multilingual vocabulary support students still building academic English, both in the room and online (Rhode Island Department of Education, n.d.).

Shared vocabulary also changes how students treat each other. When everyone has the words to describe a bug, a student can ask for help precisely instead of feeling lost, and partners talk about the code rather than about who is smart. Classrooms where students talk, listen, read, and write about real problems expose them to several viewpoints and teach them to share respectfully (Mometrix Academy, 2022). Because the room also holds questions as well as answers, curiosity becomes normal: "I don't know yet, let's check" is what students will hear from me first. Showing each student's work gives them something to be proud of (Pratiwi et al., 2019), and that pride makes the classroom feel safer for taking risks. Over time these daily habits become part of the class culture.

#### Computer Science methods: curiosity, digital literacy, and media fluency

Computer science students spend much of their time reading online sources, so the information-intensive environment has to teach them to question what they find. These routines run every week, in person and online, for all students.

##### Building a Culture of Curiosity and Critical Examination of Online Resources

- **Curiosity board.** Students post questions such as "Why does this crash only on my phone?" on a board in the room and on the digital hub. Each week the class chooses one to investigate. I add my own questions and say "I don't know yet, let's find out," so not knowing is normal for everyone.

- **Check before you paste.** Before using code or facts from the web, students apply SIFT: stop, investigate the source, find better coverage, and trace the claim to its original context (Caulfield, 2019). For code, they add three checks: Does it run? Does it fit our version? Can I explain every line?

- **Read sideways.** Students learn to leave a page and see what other sources say about it, which is how professional fact-checkers work (Wineburg & McGrew, 2019). I model this weekly with a think-aloud on one forum answer, including one that looks convincing but is wrong.

- **Compare two answers.** Pairs receive two conflicting answers to the same question, then test both and argue for one using evidence. Stating a main point and supporting it with evidence builds speaking skills as well (Mometrix Academy, 2022).

- **Treat AI output as a claim.** AI-written code and explanations are claims to test, not answers. Students record the prompt, what they checked, and what they changed. The class keeps a list of errors it has caught, which rewards skepticism rather than speed.

- **Source log.** Every project includes a short log of the sources used and why each was trusted, which supports our norm of crediting any code, images, or AI help. I use it to check that these habits continue all year, in person and online.

##### Fostering Digital Literacy and Media Fluency Across Cultural and Linguistic Backgrounds

- **Start from home languages.** Students may search, read, and take notes in any language, then compare the source with an English one. Home-language terms join the word wall, because print in a child's primary language supports multicultural learners (The Access Center, 2007).

- **Language support for evaluating.** Sentence frames such as "I trust this source because..." and a glossary of search terms let students still building academic English do the same critical thinking. Mistakes in a second language are expected and accepted (The Access Center, 2007).

- **Teach attribution openly.** Expectations about copying and credit differ across school systems, and literacy itself is shaped by culture (The Access Center, 2007). I teach our rules directly, and I treat a first mistake as a learning moment, not as dishonesty.

- **Compare perspectives.** Students compare how outlets in different countries report the same technology story and ask whose voice is missing. Students who have lived in several countries become experts on context.

- **Create as well as consume.** Students make captioned screencasts, infographics, and README files for a real audience, and choose the format that fits their message. Each one begins as a spoken rehearsal with a partner, and non-text formats let students show complex thinking while their English develops.

- **Plan for unequal access.** Using data from the background survey, I provide phone-friendly pages, offline copies, and lab time, so that no student misses out because of a device or connection. Captions and adjustable text support students with disabilities too.
