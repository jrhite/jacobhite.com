### Unit 3, Activity 1: Classroom arrangements

#### Three seating arrangements and when I would use them

As a future computer science teacher, the arrangement I expect to use most is pair
pods. Pair programming is a core practice in CS. One student "drives" at the
keyboard while the other "navigates," and they swap roles on a timer. Pair pods
make that routine natural. Because I choose the pairs, I can match students on
purpose. For example, I can pair a confident coder with a student who is still
building fluency, so both have a chance to contribute (Davies, 2025). A pair is
also a manageable unit. Darren Thomas notes that larger groups bring a lot of
social energy but are the hardest to monitor, and students easily drift off task
(Educational Research Techniques, 2022). Two students still get the benefits of
collaboration and talking through problems without a table full of distractions.
The pods also leave aisles open, so I can circulate, look at screens, and help
debug (Davies, 2025). Pairs also work well for quick partner checks, like asking
each column of pairs to trace a different piece of code before we compare answers
(Renard, 2020). One drawback is that some students may need to sit alone for a
while to stay focused. When that happens, I would explain the reason and what it
takes to rejoin a partner, so the move never feels like a punishment (Davies,
2025).

Next, I would use the horseshoe or semicircle for discussion-heavy lessons. In CS,
those include debating the ethics of AI, reviewing a classmate's code, or watching
a live demo on the projector. Everyone can see the board and also make eye contact
with each other, which supports real discussion between students and the teacher
(Poorvu Center for Teaching and Learning, n.d.). Thomas calls the half circle "the
best of both worlds." The teacher keeps control of the discussion from the open end
of the U, and students can still interact with one another (Educational Research
Techniques, 2022). The Poorvu Center points out that students on the sides of the U
can get less attention, so I would make a point of calling on them (Poorvu Center
for Teaching and Learning, n.d.). The horseshoe takes a lot of floor space, and it
doesn't work well for small-group work (Renard, 2020). For bigger classes, I would
switch to a double horseshoe.

Lastly, I would use traditional rows when the task is individual. Examples include
quizzes, coding assessments, and short direct-instruction segments where all eyes
need to be on the front. Rows reduce side conversations and give every student a
clear view of the board (Davies, 2025). However, rows limit eye contact and
discussion, and students can get bored when rows become the default (Educational
Research Techniques, 2022). Students in the back rows also tend to be less engaged
(Poorvu Center for Teaching and Learning, n.d.). So I would treat rows as a short
phase of the lesson rather than the whole lesson. Thomas suggests starting class in
rows to give instructions and then breaking students into another arrangement for
the task (Educational Research Techniques, 2022). When I can't move the furniture,
I can still add interaction with Think-Pair-Share between neighbors (Poorvu Center
for Teaching and Learning, n.d.).

For all three arrangements, I see seating as a tool I can change. A layout that
works in the first month may need to change later, and changing it is a normal part
of classroom management, not a failure (Davies, 2025). This fits InTASC Standard 3,
because I would match the room to the kind of learning each lesson requires, whether
that is collaborative, discussion-based, or individual.

### Unit 4, Activity 1: Positive classroom environments

#### Method subject: the blank file is a canvas

Programming can look like the opposite of creative work, all exact syntax and
right answers. But every program starts as something that does not exist yet,
and someone has to imagine it. Creativity also depends on climate. School climate
includes the safety and relationships people feel every day (Strobel, 2023), and
a student who does not feel safe being wrong will not try an idea that might
fail.

#### Strategies to nurture creativity and creative expression

- **Let students choose the problem.** Each student will build one real
  application across the term, starting from something that actually annoys
  someone they know. Resnick (2017) names projects, passion, peers, and play as
  the conditions for creative learning, and a project a student cares about
  supplies the first two.
- **Keep the concept fixed and the product open.** Everyone has to use lists and
  loops this week, but one student might build a match-stats tracker and another
  a time-zone tool for calling family abroad.
- **Make time for play and peers.** Short "what happens if I change this?"
  sessions come before any correct solution, and students show work in progress
  to a partner while it can still change.
- **Treat bugs as ordinary.** My classroom norms already do this, because a
  student who fears a failed attempt stops experimenting.

#### Two ways I can model creativity

1. **Live-code a problem I have not solved.** I will think out loud, try an idea,
   hit a bug, throw the idea away, and try another. Students see that the first
   attempt is rarely the final one, and that an engineer with decades of
   experience still works this way.
2. **Show my own unfinished work.** I will share a real project of mine, including
   the sketches, the rejected versions, and the suggestions from an AI tool that I
   turned down, and explain why. Students see that the idea and the decision stay
   with the person, even when AI writes some of the code.

#### How my modeling shapes what students try

People learn a great deal by watching others (Bandura, 1977), so what I do with my
own mistakes teaches more than anything I say about them. If students only ever
see finished code, they conclude that good programmers do not fail, and they
start to copy rather than invent. When I throw away my own idea in front of them,
a failed attempt becomes part of the work, and trying something unusual feels
safe. So each thing I model comes with a move for students:

- **I show a failed attempt, and students keep an "idea graveyard."** Each project
  includes a file of ideas they tried and dropped, and at each demo they share one
  favorite failure and what it taught them.
- **I show the AI suggestions I rejected, and students do the same.** They annotate
  a suggestion from an AI tool, marking what they kept, changed, or threw away, and
  why.
- **I ask "what if?" out loud, and students ask it of each other.** During partner
  reviews, every student offers one "what if?" about a classmate's project.

I will know the modeling is working when project ideas get stranger, the graveyard
files get longer, and "is this right?" gives way to "what if I tried this?"

#### Using creativity to communicate ideas, knowledge, and connections

- **Ideas.** Students will demo what they built and explain it in their own
  words. A working demo, a diagram, or a storyboard also gives students still
  building academic English a way to show complex thinking. The ISTE student
  standards expect learners to express themselves creatively with digital tools
  (International Society for Technology in Education, 2024).
- **Knowledge.** Students can write a programming joke. In an online math lesson I
  observed, the class used a joke that plays on two meanings of *right* and
  *obtuse* (Moreland University, 2021). A joke about an off-by-one error only
  works if its writer understands the error.
- **Connections among concepts.** Students will draw how data moves through their
  app from the input to the database and back, then invent their own analogy for
  it. That shows me how they connect variables, functions, and storage better than
  a list of definitions would.

### Unit 4, Activity 2: Creating a positive, information-intensive environment

#### Vlog: information-intensive environments and oral language
<VideoEmbed
  src="/media/information-intensive-environment.mp4"
  poster="/media/information-intensive-environment-poster.jpg"
  title="Information-Intensive Learning Environment"
  duration="6:11"
  aspect="16 / 9"
  caption="My vlog on information-intensive environments and oral language in a secondary computer science classroom. The script follows."
/>

Hi, I'm Jacob Hite, and I'm preparing to teach secondary computer science. Our classes run in English, and our students come from many cultures. When they walk into my room, I want them surrounded by English and by code: a word wall, charts, books, programs on the walls, and people talking about all of it. That is an information-intensive environment. The Access Center (2007) explains that this kind of setting draws students into reading, writing, and talk every day, so they see what spoken and written words are for.

The most useful display in my room will be live. I will project an IDE, an Integrated Development Environment, on a large wall at all times. An IDE is one of the most information-dense tools software engineers have. It shows code, files, errors, and suggestions in one place. Because it is open and drivable, not just a picture, any student can point at something, ask what it does, and try it live. A question turns into a term they can see, say, and use.

How do print and digital texts build vocabulary? Through repeated, meaningful contact. Computing words are tricky because many are ordinary English words with exact technical meanings. "Function," "string," and "loop" all mean something different in a program. So a word like "variable" appears on the word wall with a plain-English definition, an icon, and a code example. It appears in a beginner's guide for a seventh grader and in the official Python documentation for a twelfth grader. Those levels matter. Developmentally appropriate print and digital texts mean every student can reach the same idea. The National Children's Book and Literacy Alliance (2015) also recommends reading aloud daily, a little above grade level, so students hear new words used well. Class stays in English, but students can add a translation beside a term, because seeing their first language in print supports students from many cultures (The Access Center, 2007).

Vocabulary is only part of literacy. Shanahan and Shanahan (2008) describe disciplinary literacy as the specialized reading and writing skills of each field. In computer science, I think those skills include knowing how to use the tools of the trade: reading an error message, finding an answer in the documentation, stepping through code with a debugger, and getting around an IDE. So I teach these tools directly, and the IDE on the wall lets students watch them being used every day.

This shapes both climate and culture. Climate is the safety and support students feel each day (Strobel, 2023). A teenager who has the English words to describe a bug can ask for help without embarrassment, and a student who finds the answer on the wall or in the IDE gains independence. Culture is what the class values (Strobel, 2023). Pratiwi et al. (2019) describe displays of student work and a storytelling chair in a kindergarten. For secondary students, those become a gallery of student code and a demo chair, where a student presents a working program to the class. Over time, "let's look it up" and "show us what you built" become how we do things here.

Oral language, meaning spoken English rather than a programming language, sits underneath all of this. Students hear and say words before they read and write them well. Mometrix Academy (2022) describes literacy-rich content classrooms where students listen to several viewpoints and support their points with evidence. In pair programming, students explain out loud what the code does. That spoken explanation becomes a written code comment, and later a project README. Talking rehearses writing, and reading gives students more words to talk with.

Finally, much of what computer science students read is online, so curiosity needs critical thinking. We keep a curiosity board for real questions. Students use Mike Caulfield's SIFT method to check sources (Caulfield, 2019), read sideways like the fact-checkers Wineburg and McGrew (2019) studied, and treat AI output as a claim to test. A student can start in the human language they read best, then compare with an English source, and sentence frames support students still building academic English, so everyone takes part. Thanks for watching.
