The behaviors students might show based on their needs, and the support I will
provide for each, organized using Maslow's (1943) hierarchy from basic
physiological needs to self-actualization.

**How I read behavior:** Before I respond to what a student does, I ask which need the behavior might be serving. Hamilton (2020) traces classroom misbehavior to root causes such as unmet basic needs, a weak connection with the teacher, a bid for attention or control, low confidence or missing skills, work pitched at the wrong level, and unclear limits. Maslow's (1943) hierarchy helps me sort those causes. Its first four levels are deficiency needs, which press harder the longer they go unmet, while self-actualization is a growth need that comes from wanting to develop rather than from lacking something (Cherry, 2026; McLeod, 2026). So I check the lower levels first.

**A guide, not a ladder:** I use the hierarchy as a guide, not a strict sequence. Maslow himself said that needs can appear in a different order for some people and that people feel a mix of needs at once (Kremer & Hammond, 2013), and later research found that people pursue several needs at the same time, even when basic needs are not fully met (Cherry, 2026; Hopper, 2020). Critics also point out that the model reflects Western, individualistic values (McLeod, 2026), which matters in an international classroom where many students come from more collectivist cultures. A tired student can still be reached through belonging, so I respond to the whole student in front of me, not to a level on a pyramid.
