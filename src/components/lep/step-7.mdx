Families are partners in managing the learning environment. When teachers share
information in both directions, they and families can support students more
effectively (American Federation of Teachers [AFT], 2007). In an international
school, my students' families may speak many home languages, work in different
time zones, travel often, or share custody across two homes. So I plan for
several channels, plain language, and flexible meeting times from the start,
rather than waiting for a family to seem "hard to reach" (Morin, n.d.).

#### Three tools for regular communication

| Tool | How I will use it | Why it builds the partnership |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Monthly class newsletter, plus weekly Google Classroom guardian summaries** | A short newsletter goes out in the first week of each month through the school's messaging platform. It covers current projects, upcoming deadlines, one thing families can ask their child about at home, and links to student work. Guardian summaries in Google Classroom then email families each week about upcoming and missing work. I write in plain English with no programming jargon, so the text translates well, and I post a printable PDF copy for families who prefer paper. | A newsletter shows families what their child is doing in class (Continental, 2023). Clear information in language families understand, with ideas for supporting learning at home, keeps them able to act on it (AFT, 2007). Steady, predictable updates prevent the uncertainty that sporadic contact creates (AFT, 2007). |
| **2. Two-way messaging with translation** (the school's platform, or Remind or TalkingPoints if the school has none) | In the first two weeks, every family gets an introduction from me and a short question: how do you prefer to be contacted, in which language, and what would you like to hear about? After that, every family receives at least one specific good-news message each term. When a concern comes up, I contact the family promptly, describe what I saw with facts and data, and ask what they notice at home. Every message ends with an invitation to reply. | Remind supports two-way messages without sharing a personal phone number, and TalkingPoints translates teacher messages into families' home languages and translates replies back (Continental, 2023). Leading with good news and reaching out early keeps families involved and stops small problems from growing (AFT, 2007). One-way tools and contact that only brings problems are two of the most common communication mistakes (Nelson-Danley, 2020). |
| **3. Student-led conferences and project showcases** | Twice a year, students present their code, portfolio, and learning goals to their families. Sessions run in person and over Google Meet or Zoom, with early-morning and evening slots for families in other time zones, and a recording for families who cannot attend live. Families leave one written comment or question, which I answer within a week. | Offering video meetings, flexible times, and recorded presentations removes the barriers created by work schedules and travel (Morin, n.d.). Students explaining their own work gives families a reason to talk with them about computer science at home and turns the conference into a three-way conversation. |

#### Practices I will follow, and practices I will avoid

| I will | I will avoid |
| --- | --- |
| Contact every family early in the year, before any problem arises (AFT, 2007) | Contacting families only when something goes wrong (AFT, 2007) |
| Ask each family how and in which language they want to be contacted (Morin, n.d.) | Assuming every family reads English fluently (Nelson-Danley, 2020) |
| Use a trained interpreter for meetings in another language (Morin, n.d.) | Asking a student to interpret for their own family (Morin, n.d.) |
| Include every adult with educational decision-making rights, in every household (Morin, n.d.) | Writing only to one parent, or assuming one family structure (AFT, 2007) |
| Keep a dated communication log of every contact in a private Google Sheet (Nelson-Danley, 2020) | Relying on memory when a concern, plan, or intervention needs records |
| Follow through on what I promise, and own and fix my mistakes quickly (AFT, 2007; Nelson-Danley, 2020) | Taking criticism personally or reacting defensively (Nelson-Danley, 2020) |
| Check a tool's privacy rating and the school's approval before using it with families, since Common Sense Education stopped updating its edtech reviews in 2026 and privacy ratings now come from its affiliate, Common Sense Privacy (Common Sense Media, n.d.) | Adding a new app that collects family or student data without approval |

#### When a conversation becomes difficult

If a parent is upset, I listen first and do not defend my methods right away. I
restate what I heard and name the feeling I notice, such as frustration. Once
the parent feels heard, I share my view with examples and data, and I stay open
to their ideas, because parents often know their child best. If we cannot
agree, I bring in a colleague, the principal, or the school counselor, and I
remind the parent of something specific I value in their child (Wilson, 2015).
If a parent confronts me in front of the class, I calmly acknowledge their
concern and offer a private meeting right after class or at a set time, so the
students' learning and dignity are protected. If the situation escalates, I call
the office for support. Afterward, I record the conversation in my log and tell
my mentor teacher.

#### If family communication is not permitted

If my placement does not allow me to contact families directly, I will send the
same information to the school administrator and my mentor teacher instead: a
short weekly summary of class progress, achievements, and any behavior or
attendance concerns, plus a copy of each monthly newsletter, so they can pass
it on through the school's own channels. My communication log will still
record every concern and how it was resolved.
