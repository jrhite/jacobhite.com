#### In the online classroom

<ProcedureCards items={transitionsOnline} supportsLabel="Supports for learning differences and language barriers" toggle />

**Keeping the online class live and interactive.** Online lessons change
activity every 10 to 15 minutes, moving between a short demo, a Pear Deck
question everyone answers, pair coding in breakout rooms, and a quick share-out
in the main room. Every change follows the same pattern: a warning in the chat,
one link to open, a thumbs-up when ready, and a visible timer. Students always
know what is happening next, and the changes of activity give them regular
chances to speak, type, vote, and code instead of watching a screen for a full
period.

**Supporting students with learning differences and language barriers.**
Students may struggle with transitions because of sensory processing, emotional
regulation, attention, or speech and language processing, and they benefit from
routine, structure, and predictability (Brukner, 2021). Four supports run
through every transition above. **Predictability:** every transition uses the
same signal, a visible timer, and the same steps every time. **Advance notice:**
students who find it hard to stop an activity, including many students with ADHD
or autism, get a private heads-up before the class warning, and accommodations
in IEPs and 504 plans are followed in every transition. **Language support:**
directions are given aloud and in writing, in short sentences with icons,
gestures, and hand signals. Key transition words (save, push, log out, lids
down) are on the word wall with home-language translations, students can use a
bilingual buddy, and online sessions use live captions and posted links that
work with translation tools. **Regulation and ownership:** after an energetic
activity the class takes one collective deep breath, students who need them can
use quiet fidgets, and students who struggle most with transitions are given
leadership roles such as timekeeper or materials manager, which builds their
ownership and confidence (Brukner, 2021).

**Why these transitions.** Secondary computer science students move between
whole-class, small-group, partner, independent, and unplugged work several times
in one lesson, and every transition is a chance to lose learning time. Finley
(2017) recommends getting attention, explaining the procedure, preparing
students for a start signal, and then watching that students follow it, along
with countdowns, visible timers, hand signals for requests such as restroom
breaks, and precorrection, where a student describes the steps before the class
moves. Each transition above follows that pattern. The EngageNY (2015) video
shows a teacher guiding students quietly back into small groups with little loss
of instructional time, which is the standard I am aiming for: short, calm
transitions directed with clear cues instead of raised voices. I will teach and
practice each transition during the first two weeks and have the class redo any
that run slowly, as Finley (2017) suggests, because predictable routines let
students spend more of the period learning (Evertson et al., 2003). The
technology transitions (moving into coding, "lids down", pair programming,
switching tools, Plan B, and shared equipment) address the moments when screens
and devices most often pull attention away from learning in a computer science
classroom.

#### Our group list for grades 9–12

The transitions above grew out of a list I built with my small group in our
virtual class. We worked from the whole grade band rather than any one subject,
so it covers general moments a computer lab never sees, such as rotating through
stations and getting lab materials out. Every transition uses the same pattern:
get attention, explain the steps, have a student repeat them, give a start
signal, and watch that students follow them (Finley, 2017).

**Keeping the online class engaging, live, and interactive**

- **Change activity every 10 to 15 minutes.** Move between a short demo, a poll,
  breakout work, and a share-out so students are not watching a screen for a
  full period.
- **Make everyone respond, not just volunteers.** Use polls, chat waterfalls,
  reactions, and shared whiteboards where every student answers at the same
  time.
- **Use the same transition pattern every time.** A warning in the chat, one
  link to open, a thumbs-up when ready, and a visible timer.
- **Keep groups small and roles clear.** Breakout rooms of three or four with a
  timekeeper, reporter, and note-taker.
- **Build in movement.** A one-minute stand-and-stretch break every 25 minutes
  of screen time (Brukner, 2021).

**Strategies used across all transitions, for students with learning differences**

- **Predictable routines.** The same signal, visible timer, and steps every
  time. Students who struggle with transitions often have trouble with sensory
  processing, emotional regulation, attention, or language processing, and they
  benefit from routine, structure, and predictability (Brukner, 2021).
- **Advance notice.** A private heads-up before the class warning for students
  who find it hard to stop an activity, including many students with ADHD or
  autism.
- **Practice and precorrection.** Teach and rehearse each transition in the
  first two weeks, have a student repeat the steps before the class moves, and
  redo transitions that run slowly (Finley, 2017).
- **Regulation supports.** A collective deep breath after active tasks, quiet
  fidgets for students who need them, and leadership roles such as timekeeper or
  materials manager for students who struggle most (Brukner, 2021).
- **Individual plans.** Accommodations in IEPs, 504 plans, and health plans are
  followed in every transition.

**Strategies used across all transitions, for students facing language barriers**

- **Show and say.** Every direction is given aloud and in writing, in short
  sentences with icons, gestures, or hand signals.
- **Nonverbal requests.** Hand signals for restroom, help, and supplies, so
  students do not have to ask aloud in English (Finley, 2017).
- **Key transition vocabulary.** Words such as save, line up, pass, and log out
  are on the word wall with home-language translations.
- **Peer support.** A buddy who shares a language where possible, especially for
  newcomers.
- **Written online supports.** Live captions, pinned instructions, and clickable
  links that work with translation tools.

**Why clear transitions matter.** High school students change activities several
times in one period, and every transition is a chance to lose learning time.
Clear beginnings and endings protect instructional time (Finley, 2017). The
EngageNY (2015) example shows a teacher guiding students quietly back into small
groups with little loss of instructional time, which is the standard our group
set for these transitions: short, calm, and directed with clear cues.
