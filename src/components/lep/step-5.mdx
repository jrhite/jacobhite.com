Creating an information-intensive environment, with print and digital texts that
are developmentally appropriate for my students, can enhance vocabulary and
contribute to a positive classroom climate. These are the steps I will take.

1. **Computing word wall.** Key terms (variable, loop, conditional, function, debug) each have a plain-English definition, an icon, and a short code example. Students can add the term in their home languages next to the English.
2. **Anchor charts at eye level.** How to read an error message, our help routine, pair programming roles, and our class norms, so students can find answers without waiting for me.
3. **Classroom library of print and digital texts.** Beginner coding guides at different reading levels, graphic novels and biographies of computing pioneers from many countries, and short tech news articles, with digital copies linked in Google Classroom.
4. **Rotating student work gallery.** Printed, commented code snippets and project screenshots with QR codes that open the working program.
5. **Digital resource hub.** A Google Classroom page with language documentation, captioned tutorial videos, worked examples, and an error-message glossary, available in class, at home, and during online learning.
6. **Levels of text for grades 7–12.** Younger students start with illustrated guides and short tutorials. Older students move to official documentation and technical articles. I will rotate the texts by project and by student interest, and include both informational and literary choices (The Access Center, 2007; Just Right Reader, n.d.).
7. **Daily oral language.** Each lesson includes a two-minute read-aloud of code or a short article, and I think aloud as I read. Pairs say what the code does before they write a comment, so spoken explanations become written ones (The Access Center, 2007; National Children's Book and Literacy Alliance, 2015). Online, this happens in breakout rooms or short voice recordings.
8. **Places to share and be heard.** Pratiwi et al. (2019) describe message centers, displays of student work, and a storytelling chair in a kindergarten. I will adapt these for secondary students as a class question board, the gallery above, and a "demo chair" where a student presents a program to the class.

#### How it will affect classroom climate

An information-rich room tells students that help is always available and that they are expected to use it. When a student is stuck on an error, the anchor chart and glossary let them take the first step on their own, which builds independence and lowers the anxiety of asking in front of peers. The IRIS Center (2021b) notes that posting expectations where everyone can see them lets teachers redirect quickly and positively. Representation matters too: when the library and word wall reflect computing pioneers and languages from around the world, students in an international school can see themselves in the field, which culturally responsive teaching identifies as key to belonging and engagement (Will & Najarro, 2022). Displaying student code makes their work part of the room, so the space feels like it belongs to everyone (Williams, 2024). Captioned videos and multilingual vocabulary support students still building academic English, both in the room and online (Rhode Island Department of Education, n.d.).

Shared vocabulary also changes how students treat each other. When everyone has the words to describe a bug, a student can ask for help precisely instead of feeling lost, and partners talk about the code rather than about who is smart. Classrooms where students talk, listen, read, and write about real problems expose them to several viewpoints and teach them to share respectfully (Mometrix Academy, 2022). Because the room also holds questions as well as answers, curiosity becomes normal: "I don't know yet, let's check" is what students will hear from me first. Showing each student's work gives them something to be proud of (Pratiwi et al., 2019), and that pride makes the classroom feel safer for taking risks. Over time these daily habits become part of the class culture.

#### Computer Science methods: curiosity, digital literacy, and media fluency

Computer science students spend much of their time reading online sources, so the information-intensive environment has to teach them to question what they find. These routines run every week, in person and online, for all students.

##### Building a Culture of Curiosity and Critical Examination of Online Resources

- **Curiosity board.** Students post questions such as "Why does this crash only on my phone?" on a board in the room and on the digital hub. Each week the class chooses one to investigate. I add my own questions and say "I don't know yet, let's find out," so not knowing is normal for everyone.

- **Check before you paste.** Before using code or facts from the web, students apply SIFT: stop, investigate the source, find better coverage, and trace the claim to its original context (Caulfield, 2019). For code, they add three checks: Does it run? Does it fit our version? Can I explain every line?

- **Read sideways.** Students learn to leave a page and see what other sources say about it, which is how professional fact-checkers work (Wineburg & McGrew, 2019). I model this weekly with a think-aloud on one forum answer, including one that looks convincing but is wrong.

- **Compare two answers.** Pairs receive two conflicting answers to the same question, then test both and argue for one using evidence. Stating a main point and supporting it with evidence builds speaking skills as well (Mometrix Academy, 2022).

- **Treat AI output as a claim.** AI-written code and explanations are claims to test, not answers. Students record the prompt, what they checked, and what they changed. The class keeps a list of errors it has caught, which rewards skepticism rather than speed.

- **Source log.** Every project includes a short log of the sources used and why each was trusted, which supports our norm of crediting any code, images, or AI help. I use it to check that these habits continue all year, in person and online.

##### Fostering Digital Literacy and Media Fluency Across Cultural and Linguistic Backgrounds

- **Start from home languages.** Students may search, read, and take notes in any language, then compare the source with an English one. Home-language terms join the word wall, because print in a child's primary language supports multicultural learners (The Access Center, 2007).

- **Language support for evaluating.** Sentence frames such as "I trust this source because..." and a glossary of search terms let students still building academic English do the same critical thinking. Mistakes in a second language are expected and accepted (The Access Center, 2007).

- **Teach attribution openly.** Expectations about copying and credit differ across school systems, and literacy itself is shaped by culture (The Access Center, 2007). I teach our rules directly, and I treat a first mistake as a learning moment, not as dishonesty.

- **Compare perspectives.** Students compare how outlets in different countries report the same technology story and ask whose voice is missing. Students who have lived in several countries become experts on context.

- **Create as well as consume.** Students make captioned screencasts, infographics, and README files for a real audience, and choose the format that fits their message. Each one begins as a spoken rehearsal with a partner, and non-text formats let students show complex thinking while their English develops.

- **Plan for unequal access.** Using data from the background survey, I provide phone-friendly pages, offline copies, and lab time, so that no student misses out because of a device or connection. Captions and adjustable text support students with disabilities too.
