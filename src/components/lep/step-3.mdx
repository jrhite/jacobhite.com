#### How these routines support every learner

**A safe, caring, respectful, and productive environment for students with
exceptionalities.** My routines are designed so students with exceptionalities,
including students with disabilities, students with attention or anxiety needs,
English learners, and advanced learners, know what to do and can succeed without
being singled out. Each procedure is posted as a short numbered list with icons,
so students who struggle with working memory or reading in English can check the
next step on their own instead of waiting for help, and every routine can be
completed on a school device and account. Safety comes from privacy: the private
help channel, the “what I tried” checklist, optional cameras online, and private
corrections let students with anxiety or learning differences ask for help and
make mistakes without public embarrassment. Respect and care come from looking
for the reason behind a missed routine before responding, especially when a
disability or family circumstance affects something like arriving on time (The
IRIS Center, 2021a). Productivity comes from building supports into the routines
themselves, such as the 7-minute transition warning, starter templates, shorter
checklists, and one-on-one walkthroughs, and from following each student’s
individual support plan (such as an IEP) within every routine. Advanced learners
extend the same routines by serving as privacy buddies or sign-out checkers.

**How expectations are taught, reinforced, and maintained.** I will teach
expectations explicitly during the first two weeks. For each routine, I
introduce the steps, discuss why they matter, model examples and non-examples,
have students practice before the routine is needed, and review it often (Cox,
2019; The IRIS Center, 2021a). Students with exceptionalities get extra
rehearsal, a visual copy of the steps at their desk, and, when helpful, a
private preview before the class practices. I will reinforce expectations with
behavior-specific praise tied to the step a student completed (The IRIS Center,
2021a), and when a step is missed, I will kindly correct it and have the student
redo only that part (Kardamis, 2016). To maintain expectations, I will respond
the same way across students and over time (Evertson et al., 2003), reteach a
routine whenever several students struggle with it or after a long break, and
review how each routine is working during the year to decide where to add or
remove structure (Edutopia, 2022).

**Predictability, engagement, and positive interactions.** Every lesson opens
and closes the same way, the online class follows the same steps, and class
information lives in one place, so students always know what comes next and
where to look (Zimny, 2020). Classrooms with predictable procedures have lower
rates of challenging behavior, and explicitly taught procedures help students
monitor their own behavior (The IRIS Center, 2021a). This predictability
especially helps students with autism, ADHD, or anxiety, for whom unexpected
change can be a barrier to learning. The routines also keep students engaged:
the Do Now starts learning in the first minute, the help queue keeps students
coding while they wait, and the exit ticket gives every student a voice at the
end of class. Finally, they build positive interactions. The greeting at the
door and the online check-in start each class with personal contact, privacy
buddies and pair roles give students structured ways to help each other, and
caring routines carried into the online classroom help every student feel
welcome and valued (Zimny, 2020).

#### Why these routines and procedures

**Choosing the routines.** The IRIS Center (2021a) advises keeping procedures
simple and building them for the less structured parts of class, where problems
are most likely, and suggests asking why, where, what, who, when, and how for
each one. In a computer lab, those moments are entering, getting help while
coding, and packing up, when time and devices are most easily lost. The same
three appear on the Milwaukee Public Schools PBIS (2014) planning form for
classroom procedures. Edutopia (2022) describes the cycle I will follow:
identify where a routine is needed, build in time to demonstrate and practice it
during the first weeks, then assess it over time and decide where to add or
remove structure.

**Teaching them and responding when students don’t follow them.** I will teach
each routine by explaining it, practicing it, and reinforcing it until it
becomes automatic (Cox, 2019), following the IRIS Center’s (2021a) sequence of
introduce, discuss, model, practice, and review, and posting each one as a
numbered list. Correction is part of teaching. Kardamis (2016) recommends kindly
correcting the part done wrong, having students redo just that part, and
praising them when they get it right, which is the first response in my
modifications column. I will pair this with behavior-specific praise (The IRIS
Center, 2021a) and respond the same way every time, since uneven enforcement
leaves students unsure what is expected (Evertson et al., 2003). When a student
keeps missing a routine, I will look for the reason first. Andrew Kwok explains
that lateness and similar behaviors often have causes outside a student’s
control, and that understanding them makes classroom management more culturally
responsive (The IRIS Center, 2021a), which matters in an international school
where students arrive with different expectations about school.

**Online parallels.** The online routines keep the same steps so students do not
have to relearn them when they move between settings. Zimny (2020) recommends
using the same few routines repeatedly, keeping information in one place, and
creating a shared question page that students check before asking. Walton (2020)
adds that teachers cannot assume students know how to learn online, so routines
should be modeled and practiced, login details shared privately, and students
reminded to sign out to protect their privacy. Allowances for different devices,
connections, and time zones keep the routines fair for every student (Rhode
Island Department of Education, n.d.).

**Personal data, digital identity, and privacy.** In a computer science class,
protecting data is both content and routine. The ISTE Standards for Students ask
students to manage their digital identity and protect their privacy and personal
data online (International Society for Technology in Education, 2024). The CSTA
standards ask secondary students to describe tradeoffs between keeping
information public and private (2-IC-23), explain privacy concerns in automated
data collection (3A-IC-29), weigh the implications of privacy for safety, law,
and ethics (3A-IC-30), and recommend and compare security measures (3A-NI-06,
3A-NI-07; Computer Science Teachers Association, 2017). I will model these
skills before asking students to practice them. The Monday think-aloud follows
the same introduce, model, and practice sequence I use for every procedure (The
IRIS Center, 2021a), the Friday Data Check turns that practice into a weekly
habit, and the privacy-by-design steps apply the same habits to the programs
students write. The digital identity expectations build on Common Sense
Education’s (n.d.) focus on how online actions last and reach unseen audiences.
My own procedures follow the Privacy Technical Assistance Center’s (2014) advice
to know which online services are in use, review even free click-through tools
before adopting them, and be open with families about how student information is
used. Every routine can be completed on a school device and account, uses
plain-language materials that can be translated, and leaves students in control
of what they share. This way, students without home technology, English
learners, students with disabilities, and students whose families hold different
expectations about privacy can all take part fully (Rhode Island Department of
Education, n.d.; Will & Najarro, 2022).
