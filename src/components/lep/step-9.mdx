**Date:** November 16, 2026 · **Duration of intervention:** 8 school weeks (November 16, 2026, to January 22, 2027) · **Student:** Nancy · **Class/grade:** Grade 8 Computer Science

**About Nancy.** Nancy is a 13-year-old Grade 8 student. For this plan I place her in my Grade 8 computer science class at the international school where I plan to teach, and treat her family as one of the many expatriate families there. Her teachers describe her as hard-working and cooperative. She enjoys arts and crafts, and drawing, music, and swimming help her relax. Her psychoeducational testing showed average cognitive abilities. Her difficulties with finishing work, focus, organization, impulsivity, mood, and anxiety about speaking in public and meeting new people have been present since she started school, and her anxiety about school has grown this year (Moreland University, n.d.). So I read her behaviors as signs of skills she has not built yet, not as unwillingness. Ablon argues that common explanations for challenging behavior are often wrong, and that adults help most by teaching the skills these students are missing, such as flexibility, frustration tolerance, and problem solving (TEDx Talks, 2014). Each numbered behavior below lines up with the goal, replacement behavior, and monitoring method of the same number.

#### Disruptive behaviors

**1. Leaving coding work unfinished and unsubmitted.** Nancy starts tasks but often does not finish them or push her code to GitHub Classroom by the end of class, and homework is often late or missing. Her teachers name homework as an ongoing issue, and she says she cannot finish on time because she "can never focus" (Moreland University, n.d.). _Likely purpose:_ escaping a task that feels too long to finish.

**2. Off-task screen use during independent coding.** A few minutes into independent work, she opens other tabs, such as social media (she uses Tumblr), music, or drawing sites, and stays there until redirected, and nearby classmates start watching her screen. Inattention and distractibility have been noted since she started school (Moreland University, n.d.). _Likely purpose:_ escaping effortful work and getting something she enjoys.

**3. Impulsive calling out and task-hopping.** She calls out questions and comments during instruction, starts typing before reading the task, and jumps between parts of a project, leaving several pieces half-finished. Impulsivity is one of her long-standing difficulties (Moreland University, n.d.). _Likely purpose:_ quick relief from restlessness and an immediate answer.

**4. Losing work and missing deadlines.** She forgets to save or commit, works in the wrong copy of a project, loses files, and misses deadlines posted in Google Classroom, then has to catch up during instruction. Disorganization is part of her profile (Moreland University, n.d.). _Likely purpose:_ less a choice than a missing routine for organizing her work.

**5. Avoiding presentations and work with unfamiliar classmates.** On presentation or new-partner days, she asks to see the nurse or go to the restroom, says she is not ready, or will not share her screen with a new partner. She gets anxious when asked to speak in public or spend time with people she does not know, and her parents report more anxiety about presentations this year (Moreland University, n.d.). _Likely purpose:_ escaping situations that make her anxious.

**6. Shutting down or putting herself down when frustrated.** When code breaks or she falls behind, she may close her laptop, put her head down, snap at a partner, or say she is bad at this. She reports mood changes and irritability, with anger and frustration tied to sadness, and her parents are concerned about negative self-talk (Moreland University, n.d.). _Likely purpose:_ escaping a frustrating task and expressing distress.

#### SMART goals

**1. Finishing and submitting work.** By January 22, 2027, Nancy will push at least 4 of every 5 in-class coding checkpoints (80%) before the end of the period and submit at least 4 of 5 weekly homework tasks on time, for three weeks in a row, measured by her GitHub Classroom commit log and Google Classroom submission status.

**2. Staying on task.** By January 22, 2027, during the 20-minute independent coding block, Nancy will be on task (in the class code editor, her notes, or approved resources) in at least 8 of 10 two-minute interval checks, on at least 4 of 5 class days each week.

**3. Planning before coding.** By December 18, 2026 (week 5), Nancy will write a three-step plan or pseudocode before starting at least 4 of 5 new coding tasks, and will call out during instruction no more than once per class, measured by her plan cards and my tally.

**4. Organizing her work.** By December 11, 2026 (week 4), Nancy will complete the end-of-class "save, commit, push, plan" checklist in at least 9 of 10 classes, measured by the checklist and her commit log.

**5. Sharing her work.** By January 22, 2027, Nancy will complete at least 3 of the 4 steps on her presentation ladder, ending with a two-minute demo of her project to a small group of three classmates she has worked with, measured by the ladder tracker and her own nervousness ratings before and after each step.

**6. Coping with frustration.** By January 15, 2027 (week 7), when Nancy becomes frustrated, she will use a coping strategy (her stuck card, a private "stuck" message, or a two-minute reset) instead of shutting down or putting herself down in at least 4 of 5 recorded moments each week, measured by my ABC notes and her self-monitoring card.

**Why these goals are SMART.** Each goal names one behavior (specific), a count or percentage (measurable), a target that is challenging but reachable (achievable), a link to the problem it addresses (relevant), and a date (time-bound) (Taylor, 2024). Taylor (2024) also advises focusing goals on what the student can control, so every goal measures Nancy's own actions rather than outcomes such as grades. Week 1 (November 16 to 20, 2026) is for baseline data, and Nancy and I will confirm the final numbers together once we can see where she is starting from.

#### Target replacement behaviors

**1. Instead of leaving work unfinished: work in short sprints and push after each one.** Each task is broken into checkpoints on a checklist card. Nancy works in 10-minute timed sprints and commits and pushes after each checkpoint, so every piece of work is small enough to finish.

**2. Instead of drifting to other tabs: take a planned break.** After each sprint, she may take a two-minute break, sketching in her sketchbook or listening to one song on headphones, the kinds of activities she says help her relax (Moreland University, n.d.). She gets the break she was looking for, sooner and with less effort than waiting to be redirected.

**3. Instead of calling out and jumping in: plan first and park questions.** Before typing, Nancy writes a three-step plan or pseudocode. When a question or idea comes up during instruction, she writes it on a parking-lot sticky note or adds her name to the help queue, and I answer it at the next pause.

**4. Instead of losing work: follow the end-of-class checklist.** In the last five minutes of class, she saves, commits, pushes, and writes tomorrow's first step in her planner. At the start of class, she opens her Google Classroom to-do list.

**5. Instead of avoiding presentations: ask for a step on the ladder.** With a private signal (a card on her desk or a direct message), Nancy can ask for a lower-pressure way to share: a recorded screencast, a demo to me only, or a demo to one trusted partner. She then moves one step up the ladder every week or two.

**6. Instead of shutting down: signal "stuck" and reset.** When frustrated, she flips her stuck card or sends a private "stuck" message, takes a two-minute reset (slow breathing, sketching, or water), and then returns to the posted debugging checklist with a coping statement we wrote together: "Bugs are normal. What does the first error line say?"

**How I will teach them.** Each replacement meets the same need as the behavior it replaces (a break, relief from anxiety, or quick help), is easier to do, works at least as quickly, and is acceptable at school, which are the criteria Gregory (2025) gives for a good replacement behavior. I will teach each one with gradual release: I model it in a situation where she is likely to need it, Nancy talks me through it, she practices with me nearby giving feedback, and then she uses it on her own. We keep practicing even after she seems to know it, so the new behavior is ready when the trigger appears (Gregory, 2025). I acknowledge each use with specific, private praise, because adolescents are especially conscious of how others see them (Pfeifer & Berkman, 2018).

#### Methods to monitor

**1. Interval on-task checklist (daily).** During one 20-minute independent coding block each class, I check every two minutes whether Nancy is on task and mark it on a clipboard checklist. Ten checks give a daily on-task percentage for Goal 2.

**2. GitHub Classroom and Google Classroom records (daily, automatic).** Commit timestamps show how many checkpoints she pushed before the bell, and Google Classroom shows which homework was submitted on time. This gives objective data for Goals 1 and 4 without extra paperwork.

**3. Nancy's self-monitoring card (every sprint).** After each sprint, she rates her focus from 1 to 5, ticks her checklist items, and tallies each time she used her stuck card or a planned break. Each Friday we compare her ratings with my data, which builds the self-awareness she needs to manage her own focus.

**4. ABC notes (each incident).** For each calling-out, avoidance, or frustration moment, I record in a private sheet what happened before (antecedent), what she did (behavior), and what happened after (consequence). These notes test my guesses about each behavior's purpose, which is where choosing a replacement behavior starts (Gregory, 2025), and give the data for Goals 3 and 6.

**5. Presentation ladder tracker (each step).** Nancy and I mark each ladder step together, and she rates her nervousness from 1 to 5 before and after, so we can both see it come down with practice (Goal 5).

**6. Weekly data review (Fridays) and team reviews (weeks 4 and 8).** All the data goes into one Google Sheet with a simple chart for each goal. Every Friday, Nancy and I spend five minutes on it, celebrating one gain and choosing one adjustment. At weeks 4 and 8, the charts are the basis for the team meeting with her parents, counselor, and learning-support teacher, where we decide whether to keep, change, or fade each support.

#### Family communication

**1. Short progress message: daily for the first two weeks, then three times a week.** Through the school's messaging app, or email if the family prefers, I send two lines: one specific success and one focus for the next day. For example: "Nancy pushed all four checkpoints today and used her stuck card instead of closing her laptop. Tomorrow's focus is planning before she codes."

**2. Friday progress email with data: weekly.** Every Friday, I email both parents the one-page chart from our data sheet (on-task percentage, checkpoints pushed, homework submitted, and ladder step), one sentence from Nancy about her week, and one question about what they notice at home. Google Classroom guardian summaries also email them weekly about upcoming and missing work.

**3. Phone or video check-in: every two weeks.** A 15-minute phone or Google Meet call with both parents, at a time that works for both, to compare what we see at school and at home, especially homework routines, and to agree on one strategy to use in both places.

**4. Team meeting: weeks 4 and 8.** A meeting in person or on Google Meet with Nancy's parents, the school counselor, the learning-support teacher, and Nancy for part of it, to review the data, adjust goals, and plan how to fade supports. Nancy's input is part of every decision.

**5. Shared home-school toolkit: set up in week 1, updated weekly.** Through the school's approved platform, I share Nancy's checkpoint checklist, sprint routine, and coping statements, so her parents can use the same words and routine for homework at home. Any changes are noted in the Friday email.

**6. Same-day contact when needed.** If Nancy shows serious distress, or says something that worries me about her wellbeing, such as harsh talk about herself or her body, I follow school policy and refer her to the counselor the same day, and the counselor and I phone her parents together. I do not handle these concerns alone.

**Tone of every message.** I lead with a strength, describe behavior with facts and data, and invite the parents' view. Steady, positive, two-way contact that starts before problems grow keeps families involved (American Federation of Teachers [AFT], 2007). I never compare Nancy with her older sister, and I keep what the family shares confidential.

#### Action Plan and Follow-Through

The IRIS Center (n.d.) recommends that a behavior plan include an action plan listing goals, the tasks needed to reach them, and completion dates. Mine runs as follows.

- **Week 1 (November 16 to 20, 2026):** Collect baseline data with the interval checklist, commit log, and ABC notes. Meet Nancy one-on-one to hear how class feels to her, share what I have noticed, and choose her goals and replacement behaviors together. Make an introductory call to her parents. Review her psychoeducational report with the counselor and learning-support teacher.
- **Week 2 (November 23 to 27):** Teach the sprint routine, plan card, end-of-class checklist, and stuck card with gradual release. Start the self-monitoring card and daily messages home. Seat Nancy near me, away from high-traffic areas, with one trusted pair-programming partner.
- **Week 4 (December 7 to 11):** First team meeting. If the data show progress, move from daily to three messages a week.
- **Weeks 5 to 7 (December 14, 2026, to January 15, 2027, after winter break):** Lengthen sprints toward 15 minutes as on-task data rise, move up the presentation ladder, and give Nancy more project choice, such as a drawing app or a music playlist tool that connects coding to her interest in art and music.
- **Week 8 (January 18 to 22, 2027):** Final team review. Fade supports one at a time (for example, keep her checklist but stop my interval checks) and set the next plan.

**Reinforcement and consequences.** Reinforcement is specific, private, and quick: a word at her desk, a positive note home, and earned choice of project topic. When Nancy misses a step, my response is calm, private, and teaches the skill: an unfinished checkpoint is completed in a supported lunchtime lab, not as a punishment, and a lost file becomes a chance to practice the save routine. This follows the IRIS Center's (n.d.) guidance that consequences be immediate, protect the student's dignity, be delivered calmly and consistently, teach rather than punish, be paired with positive consequences, and relate logically to the behavior.

**What this plan does not do.** Nancy's mood changes, sleep difficulties, and her parents' concerns about body image belong with the school counselor and her family's health professionals. My role is to make computer science class a predictable, safe place for her, to teach the skills above, and to share what I see with the people supporting her.

#### How This Plan Reflects InTASC Standard 3: Learning Environments

Standard 3 asks teachers to work with others to create environments that support individual and collaborative learning and that encourage positive social interaction, active engagement in learning, and self-motivation. Each part of the standard shows up in this plan:

- **Working with others:** Nancy's parents, counselor, and learning-support teacher review the same data and agree on shared strategies, and Nancy helps design her own goals.
- **Individual learning:** Sprints, checkpoints, and checklists make independent coding manageable for a student who struggles with focus and organization.
- **Collaborative learning and positive social interaction:** One trusted pair-programming partner and the presentation ladder build her confidence with classmates step by step, instead of letting her avoid group work altogether.
- **Active engagement:** Projects connected to her love of art and music give her a reason to code.
- **Self-motivation:** Self-monitoring and the weekly data review let Nancy see her own progress and take ownership of it, so the plan builds independence instead of dependence on my reminders.

#### Computer Science Methods: Cultural Competency and Family Partnership

**The cultural contexts of my students' families.** My Grade 8 computer science class will be in an international school in Chiang Mai, Thailand, so many family cultures share one classroom. Some families are Thai, sometimes with grandparents closely involved in decisions about school. Others are expatriate families on work assignments from Europe, North America, Australia, and across Asia. Some have moved several times, so their children are third culture kids, and some are bicultural families with two home languages. Parents may work in different time zones, travel often, or share custody across two homes (see Step 7). Families also differ in what they expect from a teacher. Some see the teacher as the authority and may not question a plan or ask for a meeting, while others expect frequent updates and an active role. Views on learning support, counseling, and mental health vary too, so a behavior plan like Nancy's can feel welcome to one family and embarrassing to another. Nancy's own family is English-speaking, with two university-educated parents, a high-achieving older sister, and a mother at home who is available to meet (Moreland University, n.d.). In a family with high academic expectations, the risk is that a behavior plan sounds like a verdict, so I frame every message around growth, effort, and Nancy's strengths, never around comparison.

**How I can and will continue to develop cultural competency.**

- **Start with my own lens.** As an American who has lived in Thailand for many years, I have learned how often my own expectations about directness, time, and politeness are cultural rather than universal. When a family is quiet or does not reply, I ask what might be in the way, such as working hours, time zones, or comfort writing in English, instead of assuming they do not care (Morin, n.d.).
- **Learn from each family.** At the start of the year, every family completes a short intake, in their preferred language, about home languages, preferred contact, who makes school decisions, good times to talk, and their hopes for their child. It pairs with the student survey in Step 1.
- **Bring families' cultures into class.** Culturally responsive teaching uses students' customs, experiences, and perspectives as tools for instruction (Will & Najarro, 2022). I invite parents who use technology in their work to share it, and students build projects for their own families and communities.
- **Use the school's expertise.** I work with EAL staff, counselors, and trained interpreters, never the student, for meetings in another language (Morin, n.d.), and I ask colleagues from the communities we serve how a message is likely to be received.
- **Plan around the calendar.** I keep a calendar of major holidays across our families, such as Lunar New Year, Ramadan and Eid, Diwali, Thai New Year, and Christmas, so meetings and deadlines do not clash.
- **Reflect and keep learning.** After each family meeting, I note what worked and what I misread, ask my mentor teacher for feedback, join the school's professional development on intercultural communication, and ask families each term how communication is working for them.

**How technology eases communication for families from different cultural and linguistic backgrounds.**

- **Two-way translated messaging.** TalkingPoints, or the school's own platform, translates my messages into each family's home language and translates their replies back (Continental, 2023), so families can answer in the language they think in.
- **Plain language, with people for sensitive topics.** I write short sentences without programming jargon so machine translation stays accurate. For anything sensitive, such as a behavior plan, I use a trained interpreter instead of machine translation.
- **Showing, not just telling.** Short, captioned screencasts of students demonstrating their code, and simple progress charts, cross language barriers better than paragraphs of text. Nancy's weekly chart does the same for her parents.
- **Video meetings across time zones.** Google Meet calls, with an interpreter when needed, and a booking page that shows open times in each family's own time zone help parents who travel or work abroad take part.
- **Guardian summaries.** Google Classroom guardian summaries email families weekly about upcoming and missing work (see Step 7), so families do not depend on their child to report.
- **Privacy first.** I use only school-approved tools and check what data each one collects before inviting families to use it (see Step 7).

**A system of frequent, transparent communication that makes parents co-collaborators.**

- **A predictable rhythm.** Weekly guardian summaries, plus a Friday note for students with individual plans; a monthly class newsletter; twice-yearly student-led conferences; and a family survey each term. Families know when they will hear from me and what it will contain.
- **Transparency.** Grades, assignments, and any behavior plan are visible to families. I explain decisions with data and reasons, reply to every message within one school day, and log every contact (Nelson-Danley, 2020).
- **Two-way by design.** Every message ends with a question or an invitation to reply, and I ask what works at home before suggesting what should change. Contacting families early and with good news, before problems grow, keeps them involved (AFT, 2007).
- **Shared decisions.** For a student like Nancy, parents, student, counselor, and teacher meet at set points, review the same data, and agree on strategies for school and home, so the plan is something we build together rather than something I report.
- **Everyone included.** I include every adult with educational decision-making rights (Morin, n.d.) and offer meetings at different times and in different formats.
- **Closing the loop.** Each term, I ask families which channels and how often work best, and adjust, so the system keeps fitting the families in the room.
