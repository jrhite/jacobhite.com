Technology helps me manage the learning environment when it saves instructional
time, shows me trends in student behavior, keeps families informed, and gives
students real responsibility (Antoine et al., 2018). I chose each tool below for
a management job first and for its technology second, because lessons should be
designed around the learning objective rather than the tool (Antoine et al.,
2018). Every tool works in both my physical classroom and online, and every
digital activity has a no-tech backup for days when the network or a device
fails (Tutt, 2024).

#### Technology for the classroom

| Technology | How I will use it, in person and online | Rationale |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Google Classroom** | The class hub. Each lesson gets one post with the agenda, slides, links, tutorial videos, and assignments, and a QR code on the board opens it. Online, the same post holds the Google Meet link and the lesson recording, so absent and remote students follow the same path. Guardians receive weekly email summaries. | Keeping links, tutorials, and daily agendas in one place cuts the downtime that starts when students cannot find what they need (Tutt, 2024). Students who know where to look start faster and need fewer reminders, and guardian summaries keep a line of communication open with families (Antoine et al., 2018). |
| **2. Classroomscreen** | Projected on the board every lesson: a visual timer for each part of the lesson, work symbols (silent, whisper, partner talk), a sound-level meter during pair programming, a random name picker for questions, and a group maker that forms random pairs and teams (Classroomscreen, n.d.). Its timer runs timed partner talk, where one partner explains for a set time while the other listens and then they switch (Clowes, 2011). Wheel of Names is a lighter option for random picks. Online, I share the same screen in Meet. | Projected timers show the time limit and the time remaining, which keeps transitions tight and protects instructional time (Antoine et al., 2018; Tutt, 2024). Work symbols and the noise meter signal expectations without my raising my voice. Random pickers and random pairs give every student an equal chance to contribute and mean students work with everyone, not only their friends, which supports the positive social interaction InTASC Standard 3 calls for (Council of Chief State School Officers [CCSSO], 2013). |
| **3. GitHub Classroom** | Each assignment gives every student a private repository of starter code. Their commits show their progress between lessons. Trained student "tech leads" help classmates with Git. The workflow is identical online. | Commit history shows me who is stuck or has stopped working before they fall behind, and technology that monitors engagement lightens the work of tracking it by hand (Antoine et al., 2018). It also gives me evidence to monitor progress and guide decisions, as InTASC Standard 6 asks (CCSSO, 2013). Tech-lead roles give students leadership and real responsibility. |
| **4. Pair programming:** RallyCoach in person, Visual Studio Live Share online | In person, pairs follow RallyCoach on one keyboard and one screen: one partner drives while the other coaches, and they switch at every problem (Clowes, 2011). No extra software is needed. Online, partners open one project in Visual Studio Live Share, which lets both edit and debug the same code in real time and lets a partner join from a browser without installing anything (Microsoft, n.d.). The same rule holds online: only the driver types. Replit's real-time collaboration is an alternative, but it needs paid Core or Pro seats (Replit, n.d.). | Switching roles gives both partners a clear job, so neither one drifts off task or takes over. That is the collaborative learning and active engagement InTASC Standard 3 asks for (CCSSO, 2013). In the room, one shared keyboard keeps the turn-taking honest and lets me see at a glance who is driving as I circulate (Tutt, 2024). Live Share gives each partner their own cursor (Microsoft, n.d.), which suits remote work but makes it easy for the coach to start typing, so I use it only for online pairs and enforce the driver-only rule. Microsoft has put Live Share in maintenance mode, so its features keep working but no new ones are planned (Microsoft, n.d.), and the code stays safe in GitHub Classroom whichever editor we use. |
| **5. SchoolAI** | AI tutoring "Spaces" I build for one topic, such as recursion, with rules like "give hints, not finished code." Its Mission Control dashboard shows me each student's conversation, progress, and sentiment in real time, in person or online. | Computer science students will use AI either way. SchoolAI addresses the problem of monitoring how students use it and lets the teacher step in when needed (Gonzalez, 2025). Seeing a frustrated or stuck student in real time lets me reach them before frustration turns into off-task behavior. Students know the dashboard exists: it is a support, not a trap. |
| **6. Classroom device management** (GoGuardian Teacher or the school's own tool) | In the lab, I can see open tabs, push one link to every screen, and lock screens for "eyes up" moments. Online, it confirms that students are on the class tab. I still walk the room and use the lightest response first. | Circulating and starting with simple redirection are the first responses to off-task behavior (Tutt, 2024), and this tool extends that view to every screen. Monitoring can make students hold back their thoughts and feelings (Laird et al., 2022), so I will use it only during class, tell students when it is on, use it to spot good work as well as distraction, and follow the school's privacy rules (Privacy Technical Assistance Center, 2014). |
| **7. Google Meet and Zoom** | The online classroom: breakout rooms for pair and team work, reshuffled each round for quick new partners, plus polls, Q&A, and attendance reports. In Zoom, the host can broadcast one message to every breakout room at once, such as "Switch roles now" (Zoom, n.d.). For activities where students mingle and find partners, a spatial video space such as Gather lets students move avatars through a virtual room, with audio fading in as they come close to someone (Kobayashi et al., 2022). Meet is also where guest experts and partner classes join us (see the methods section below). | Breakout rooms bring the partner routines of the physical room online, and broadcasts and timers keep every room on the same schedule. Q&A lets students ask without interrupting, and attendance reports save time and show patterns. Breakout rooms, polls, Q&A, and attendance tracking need the Teaching and Learning or Education Plus edition of Meet (Google, n.d.), so I will check the school's edition and use Zoom, or run polls in Google Forms, if those features are missing. |
| **8. Google Forms and Sheets** | The student background survey, exit tickets, and a three-question weekly wellbeing check-in. Responses and my own notes (positive notes, missing work, help requests) flow into one Sheet that I review each week. | Technology that tracks behavior and engagement, celebrates achievement, and supports communication with parents lightens a teacher's load (Antoine et al., 2018). The weekly review shows trends, such as a student who suddenly stops submitting work, so I can respond early and share specific examples, positive and concerning, with families. |

#### Computer Science methods: authentic learning with experts, teams, and students

Computer scientists rarely work alone. They review one another's code, join
teams that span time zones, and build for real users. InTASC Standard 5 asks
teachers to engage learners in collaborative problem solving on authentic local
and global issues (CCSSO, 2013), and the ISTE Global Collaborator standard asks
students to use digital tools to work in teams locally and globally (ISTE,
2024). Pathful Connect is my main tool for bringing experts in and connecting with a
partner class abroad, and Padlet and Discord carry the same work into teams and
peer exchanges.

| Tool | What it is and how it works | How I will use it with my students |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **9. Pathful Connect** | A K–12 live, virtual work-based learning platform that connects classrooms with tens of thousands of industry professionals (Pathful, n.d.). I post a request tied to a lesson, the platform matches a professional, and that person joins my class by live video. It offers flexible live session formats, including live industry chats, and is designed to remove the geographic and resource barriers that keep many students from meeting professionals (Pathful, n.d.). It is a paid subscription, so if the school does not license it, I will run the same sessions in Google Meet with local technology companies and the school's parent and alumni network. | **Project kickoff:** before a web app unit, a user experience designer explains how she tests designs with real users, and students then write user stories for their own apps. **Code review:** midway through a project, a software engineer reviews two teams' code on screen and models how professionals give feedback. **Demo day:** professionals join remotely to watch final demos, ask questions, and give feedback. **Career range:** speakers from different countries and roles (cybersecurity, game development, data science) let students in an international school see people like themselves in computing. Before each session, students post questions on Padlet; afterward, each writes one change they will make to their project. Online students join the same session from home. **Partner class abroad:** the same live-video setup, in Google Meet, links us with a partner class in another country. Mixed teams share one GitHub Classroom repository, rotate roles (lead, tester, documenter, reviewer), and review each other's code between sessions, for example to build an app that compares air-quality data from both cities. Because the coding happens between calls, time zones matter less. |
| **10. Padlet** | A shared online board where students post text, links, images, and short videos. Gonzalez (2025) recommends it for collecting student video posts now that Microsoft has retired Flip. | Partner classes post 60-second video introductions and project demos and comment on each other's work. A "questions for our expert" board prepares students for Pathful Connect sessions, and guest experts leave written feedback after demo day. It also handles partner work outside class: one student records a timed explanation and a partner records a reply. |
| **11. Discord** (a teacher-owned class server) | A chat platform with text channels, voice and video channels, and screen sharing. Users must be at least 13, or older where local law requires (Discord, 2025). Server moderators can turn on AutoMod, which blocks chosen keywords and dangerous links and flags harmful messages, and teen accounts have sensitive-content filters turned on by default (Discord, n.d.). | **Help and showcase channels:** students post an error message in #help and classmates answer before I do, and finished projects go in #showcase. **Team channels:** each project team gets a text channel and a voice channel for short online stand-ups. **Global partners:** the partner class joins a shared server for our joint project, so teams can talk between time zones. **Expert follow-up:** after a Pathful Connect session, the guest answers further questions in an "ask me anything" channel. Server rules repeat our class norms, AutoMod is on, and I moderate. I will check the school's policy first and use the server only with students aged 13 and older whose families agree. Everyone else gets the same help and showcase spaces in the Google Classroom stream, so no one misses out. |

#### Making the tools work together

- **Routines first.** Each tool is introduced and practiced as a routine before
  it carries content, because expectations for devices need to be in place
  before tech-rich lessons begin (Tutt, 2024). I add tools a few at a time and
  keep more than one option ready, since a strategy that works in one setting
  may not work in another (Antoine et al., 2018).
- **Create, don't just consume.** Off-task behavior is more common when students
  only consume content, so most of these tools ask students to make something:
  code, a poll answer, a video, a review (Tutt, 2024).
- **A plan B for every lesson.** Every digital activity has an analog version,
  such as printed code, whiteboards, or paper exit tickets, plus a list of
  approved tasks for students who finish early (Tutt, 2024).
- **Privacy and transparency.** Students use school accounts, I collect only the
  data I need, and families learn which tools we use and why (Privacy Technical
  Assistance Center, 2014; Laird et al., 2022).
- **Clear turns for collaboration.** Timers, random pairing, breakout rooms,
  and shared editors all support the same partner routines in person and
  online, with set turns and switching roles. Clowes (2011) links routines like
  these to higher engagement, better social skills, and fewer discipline
  problems.
- **Built with others.** Experts, partner classes, families, and student tech
  leads all help run this classroom, which is what InTASC Standards 3 and 10
  ask for: learning environments built with others, and collaboration with
  learners, families, and community members (CCSSO, 2013).
